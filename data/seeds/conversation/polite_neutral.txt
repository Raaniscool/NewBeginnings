Mr. Hale: Excuse me — is this the queue for platform six tickets?
Stranger: It is, yes. Well, sort of. There are two queues and they've merged in the middle. I think the left half is now platform six and the right half is general inquiries, but the line between them is rather philosophical at this point.
Mr. Hale: I appreciate the philosophy. I'm not in a hurry — my train isn't until twenty past.
Stranger: You should be fine, then. The announcement said there's a slight delay on the six o'clock from this platform, so the crowd thickens every five minutes or so.
Mr. Hale: Was the delay announced as five minutes or forty-five? Last time an announcement said "slight," I was reading for an hour and a half and finished a chapter and a half.
Stranger: They said ten minutes, which in railway terms is somewhere between twelve and an apology.
Mr. Hale: You've done this before.
Stranger: Commuter, for my sins. Two stops to the city on a good day, and the occasional half-day adventure when the points fail north of the river junction. I'm Nora, by the way.
Mr. Hale: Roger. Well, Nora, while we stand here in philosophical territory, may I ask — is the coffee at that little kiosk worth joining a second queue for, or is the vending machine the wiser investment?
Nora: The kiosk, no question. The machine does something to coffee that should be banned under international law, whereas Rosa at the kiosk — that's her there in the red apron — makes a cup that respects you as a person.
Mr. Hale: High praise. Then when I reach the front, I'll buy two — as a bribe for the queue's patience holding my place.
Nora: Accepted, shamelessly. And if the ten minutes turns into forty-five, I'll let you in on the view from platform four — that's where the replacement buses pretend not to be.

Librarian: Can I help you find anything?
Visitor: Yes, hello. I'm looking for something to read that isn't a mistake. I know that's vague.
Librarian: It's the best kind of vague. What was the last book you liked?
Visitor: A biography of a mountaineer. It was sad, but in a good way — like eating very good soup in the rain.
Librarian: Hmm. And what do you feel like now — more sad soup, or are you ready for something warmer?
Visitor: Warmer. Not cheerful exactly; I don't think I'd trust cheerful after the mountaineer. Just... something where people are decent to each other for three hundred pages.
Librarian: I have exactly the shelf. Upstairs, second room, on the left — the light blue spines, those are the venerable gentle novels, everything set around villages and committees, no one gets murdered except reputationally. But a warning: two of them will make you want to bake bread.
Visitor: I already bake bread.
Librarian: Then you're ready. Would you like a recommendation to start with?
Visitor: Please.
Librarian: "The Garland Season" — it's the one with the small stone library on the cover. The town in it finds out its old well is the only water left after a drought, and has to behave. Not a page is wasted.
Visitor: "Has to behave" — that's a good summary of most of human history, isn't it?
Librarian: It is, but most of human history doesn't have a happy ending and a village fête at the end of it. First floor, on the left.
Visitor: Thank you — I mean it. You just saved my evening.
Librarian: That's my evening too, then, in a way. Bring it back in three weeks, won't you? The next person is waiting in spirit.
