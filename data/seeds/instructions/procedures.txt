How to Change a Bicycle Inner Tube

You will need: two tire levers, a spare tube or a repair patch, a pump, and a calm ten minutes.

1. Turn the bicycle upside down onto its handlebars and saddle, or hang it. Loose the brakes if they reach across the rim.
2. Let out whatever air remains in the tire. Press the small pin in the middle of the valve and listen; no more hiss means you may proceed.
3. Push the bead of the tire away from the rim all around with your thumbs, working both sides.
4. Slip one lever under the bead and hook it onto a spoke. Slip the second lever in five centimeters away, and walk it around the rim until one whole side of the tire is free.
5. Pull out the inner tube, but leave the valve in its hole for a moment. Find what punctured it — inflate the old tube a little and listen, or look for the tell-tale hole — then check the inside of the tire with a slow, careful finger for a thorn or a shard of glass. This is the step everyone skips and the reason everyone repairs two tubes.
6. Inflate the new tube just until it holds its shape. Tuck it into the tire, valve first, then push the bead of the tire back over the rim by hand, finishing, if you must, with the gentle tool.
7. Go around the wheel and make sure no inch of tube is trapped under the bead. Pump the tire to the pressure printed on its side.
8. Spin the wheel and watch the tire run true, without wobbles. Remount the brakes, turn the bicycle right side up, and throw nothing away that you brought out — the next repair will use the levers again.

How to Read a Weather Forecast Like an Adult

Forecasts are far more useful than their reputation, if read in the right order. Here is the order.

First, read the wind. Wind tells you about change before anything else: a swing from south to north means different air is coming, and most weather is really just a change of air.

Second, read the pressure trend if it is given. Pressure is the weather's heartbeat. Falling pressure means unsettled weather coming; rising pressure means settled weather arriving. Speed matters: a slow slide means days of rain to come, while a fast fall means a short but energetic visit.

Third, read the rain probability for what it is: a probability. Sixty percent rain means that on six out of ten days like this, rain falls somewhere in the area — not that six-tenths of your garden will be rained on. An umbrella entered a relationship with statistics long ago and should not take it personally.

Fourth, read the temperature last, because temperature is the thing the forecast tells you most confidently and you misunderstand least. The 'feels like' number is the wind's opinion of the temperature, and it is the one that decides your coat.

Finally: never blame the forecaster for being wrong about the afternoon of a hard-to-predict day. The forecast is a map drawn by a scout ahead of an army; it is the job of a scout to be approximately right. Generals who trust scouts blindly, or not at all, lose the same battles. Trust the tendency; prepare for the surprise.

How to Save Seeds from a Tomato

Seed saving is one of the small traditions by which gardeners befriend the future. For tomatoes it is not difficult, but it is particular, and the particulars are the whole craft.

1. Choose a ripe, healthy tomato from the plant that fed you best, not the first of the season and not the biggest. You are choosing a mother for next year's plants; choose the plant with the character you want to see again.
2. Cut the tomato and squeeze the seeds, with their slippery jelly, into a small glass. Eat or cook with the rest, which has done its work.
3. Add a little water and leave the glass on the kitchen counter for two or three days, away from direct sun. A thin white foam will form on top: this is fermentation, not rot, and it is the point. The good bacteria dissolve the jelly that would otherwise keep the seed asleep and carry the diseases of this year into next.
4. When the foam has done its work and the good seeds have sunk to the bottom, pour off everything floating at the top with care. Fill the glass with fresh water, swirl, let it settle again, and pour again. Repeat until the water runs clean.
5. Spread the clean seeds on a china plate or a coffee filter in a warm airy place out of the sun. Do not use paper towel; it glues the seeds to itself with a satisfaction only it understands.
6. After a week of being moved about daily to dry evenly, the seeds are ready. They should feel like tiny wooden beads, not like paper.
7. Label an envelope with the variety and the year, put in the seeds, and store the envelope somewhere cool, dark, and dry. A jar with a spoonful of dry rice tucked in makes an excellent moisture guard.
8. In spring, sow the seeds a finger's width below the surface of warm soil, and give them light. Remember: every seed you save knows everything the season taught it; you are not storing a plant, you are storing a lesson.

The Rules of Chess Club, Pinned to the Wall Since 1987

1. Shake hands before you begin and when you finish. If you cannot be nice about chess, you will not be allowed to be good at it here.
2. Touch a piece, move the piece, if the move is legal. If your hand twitches, that was a touch. We have all been young.
3. Clocks: press with the same hand that moved. Do not hover. Do not lean. Your time is your own property.
4. Talking analysis over someone else's game is the one crime we do not forgive twice in a night. If the kibitzer cannot stop themselves, the kibitzer can find another board to bother.
5. Games are adjourned when the next table needs you, and adjudicated Wednesdays by whoever holds the ladder's top three places. Decisions from the adjudicators are final, except when clearly wrong, in which case buy them a coffee and prove your variation.
6. New players of any age get the first three Tuesdays free, a partner for every game, and absolutely no special treatment after Week Three. The ladder does not care that you are new, and that is why it is honest.
7. Coffee is made by the roster on the wall. Whoever is lowest on the ladder makes it next.
8. The club's one-clock-delay in the last round belongs to the oldest player present, and he may wave it at a slow opponent twice. This is the only rule whose origin nobody remembers. It stands.

How to Write a Job Application That People Actually Read

The people who read applications read fifty of them and are hoping, honestly, to put forty-eight down unread. Your cover letter's job is not to convince. It is to refuse to be put down.

Address it to someone, not to 'Sir or Madam.' A name is findable. Five minutes on the company website or a telephone call to the firm's reception will produce one, and the name alone puts your letter into the smallest of the three piles.

Begin with them, not with you. 'Your job posting for a night porter interests me because I have spent six years being the only person awake who knows how to restart the boiler' is five times as strong as 'I am writing to apply for the aforementioned position.'

Keep it to one page. The reader is not keeping you from anything; she is keeping her afternoon. Three paragraphs: why you, why them, and the evidence of your last position, with numbers if the honest numbers help.

The most common mistake is adjectives. 'I am a dynamic team player' means 'I have not yet learned that evidence beats praise.' Replace every adjective about yourself with a past event that makes the adjective unnecessary. Hardworking = 'when the freezer failed at 2 a.m. I drove in without being asked.'

End with a specific forward step: 'I can come for an interview any Thursday or Friday next month' is superior to anything with 'opportunity,' and clearly beats 'looking forward to hear from you soon.' The person who decides when to hear from you has her own calendar; offer yours and be done.

How to Pack a Wound Dressing: First Aid for Deep Scrapes and Cuts

Good wound care is mostly about doing a few things properly and the rest hardly at all. This procedure is for minor deep scrapes and cuts; anything that won't stop bleeding, gapes widely, or follows a dirty object deserves the doctor instead.

1. Wash your hands first. There is no second step more important than this first one.
2. Rinse the wound with cool running water for at least two minutes. Talk through it if the wounded person is small, and count the seconds out loud; counting is an anesthetic older than aspirin.
3. Surround the wound with soapy water, but keep soap out of the wound itself: soap is for the skin that got hurt, not for the hurt.
4. Pat dry with a clean cloth, always from the wound outward. Do not blow on it; whatever lives in your breath does not belong in someone else's bloodstream.
5. Coat a thin layer of plain petroleum jelly or a wound ointment over the injury. The tradition of letting wounds 'breathe' robs them of the moisture that new skin requires.
6. Cover with a sterile non-stick dressing taped down on all sides, snug, but not tight. A bandage that cuts off circulation is a complication in an adhesive wrapper.
7. Change the dressing daily, or whenever it becomes wet or dirty, and use the change to look: healing skin is pink and slightly itchy. Spreading redness, warmth, swelling, or pus means the conversation with the doctor begins today instead.
8. Keep the wounded part elevated above the heart for the first day if there is swelling. Gravity assists many quiet repairs and is always available.
