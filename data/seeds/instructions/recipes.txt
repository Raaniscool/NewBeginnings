Lentil Soup for a Cold Day (Serves Six)

This is the soup that gets made in our house on the first truly cold evening of autumn, and it has never once failed to improve the household mood.

Ingredients:
2 tablespoons of oil
1 large onion, chopped fine
2 carrots, chopped into small coins
2 sticks of celery, sliced thin
3 cloves of garlic, crushed
1 and a half cups of red lentils, rinsed
2 liters of water or light stock
1 teaspoon of ground cumin
half a teaspoon of paprika
salt and pepper
a squeeze of lemon at the end

Method:
1. Warm the oil in a big pot over medium heat. Add the onion with a pinch of salt and let it soften for five minutes, stirring now and then, until it turns translucent and sweet-smelling.
2. Add the carrots and celery and let them join the onion for another five minutes. The vegetables should relax, not brown.
3. Add the garlic, cumin, and paprika and stir for thirty seconds, just until the spice becomes fragrant. Be careful here; burned garlic is bitter and holds grudges.
4. Pour in the lentils and the water, bring everything to a gentle boil, then lower the heat, cover the pot halfway, and let it simmer for twenty-five minutes. Skim off any foam in the first few minutes.
5. When the lentils have surrendered and gone soft and golden, add salt and pepper to taste. Squeeze in the lemon, taste once more, and adjust.
6. Serve hot with bread. If you like it partly smooth, press some of the lentils against the side of the pot with the back of your spoon before serving.

The soup keeps for three days in the refrigerator and actually improves overnight, which makes it one of the few dishes that plans ahead better than most cooks.

Oat Bread That Forgives Beginners

This bread asks for no kneading skill and very little patience; it is the bread I teach to people who are afraid of bread.

Ingredients:
500 grams of bread flour, plus a little extra for the bowl
7 grams of dried yeast
1 teaspoon of salt
1 tablespoon of honey
300 milliliters of warm water — warm to your finger, not hot
3 tablespoons of oats, a little extra for the top
2 tablespoons of oil

Method:
1. In your largest bowl, combine the flour, salt, yeast, and oats. Keep the salt and yeast on opposite sides of the bowl at first, because salt bullies yeast when they meet undiluted.
2. Stir the honey and oil into the warm water and pour the liquid into the flour. Mix with a wooden spoon until a rough, shaggy dough forms. It will look untidy; that is expected.
3. Cover the bowl with a clean towel and let the dough rise in a draft-free place for about an hour, or until it has doubled in size and holds a gentle wobble when you shake the bowl.
4. Scrape the dough onto a floured board and fold it over on itself ten or twelve times, gently, to build a smooth round. You are not punching it; you are folding laundry.
5. Turn the round seam-side down, put it on a lined baking tray, cover again, and let it rise another forty minutes while the oven heats to its hottest setting.
6. Sprinkle a few oats on top, make one confident slash across the dome with a sharp knife, and put the bread in. Turn the oven down at once to moderately hot.
7. Bake thirty to thirty-five minutes until the crust is deep brown and the loaf sounds hollow when you knock its bottom.
8. Cool on a rack for at least an hour before cutting. Warm bread is a temptation and hot bread is a trap; the crumb finishes its work as it cools.

Rice Pudding, Slow and Proper

Rice pudding is a lesson in patience disguised as a dessert, and it rewards exactly the cook who does nothing much to it for two hours.

Ingredients:
1 liter of whole milk
4 tablespoons of short-grain rice
3 tablespoons of sugar
a pinch of salt
half a vanilla pod or a teaspoon of vanilla extract
a nutmeg for grating
a knob of butter

Method:
1. Heat the oven to the cooler side of moderate. Butter a baking dish.
2. Rinse the rice in a sieve under cold water just once — you want to keep its starch, which is the pudding's quiet glue.
3. Pour the milk into a saucepan, add the rice, sugar, salt, and the split vanilla pod with its seeds scraped in, and warm it gently until it is just under a simmer.
4. Pour everything into the baking dish, drop the knob of butter onto the surface, and grate a whisper of nutmeg over it all.
5. Bake uncovered for one and a half to two hours, stirring gently every thirty minutes for the first hour, then leaving it alone to form its golden skin.
6. The pudding is done when the rice is completely tender and the milk has thickened into a sauce that flows slowly off a spoon. The skin on top is not a mistake; in this house it is the prize.
7. Let it rest for ten minutes before serving. It is one of those foods that is at its best slightly warmer than the room, spreading calm as it cools.

Honest Chicken Stew

A stew is the kitchen's answer to most of life's problems, and this one has the further advantage of liking to be left alone.

Ingredients:
1 chicken, jointed into eight pieces, or eight chicken thighs
2 tablespoons of flour, seasoned with salt and pepper
2 tablespoons of oil
2 onions, sliced
3 carrots, in thick pieces
2 potatoes, in chunks
4 cloves of garlic, unpeeled
1 liter of light chicken stock
2 bay leaves
a sprig of thyme if you have it
salt and pepper
a handful of peas to finish

Method:
1. Pat the chicken pieces dry and roll them in the seasoned flour.
2. In a heavy pot, brown the chicken in the oil in two batches, skin side down first. Do not crowd the pot, and do not fiddle with the pieces; three undisturbed minutes makes the color.
3. Set the chicken aside. In the same pot, soften the onions, scraping up all the brown treasures the chicken left behind.
4. Add the carrots and potatoes, return the chicken and any resting juices to the pot, add the garlic cloves, bay, thyme, and enough stock to come nearly level with the ingredients.
5. Bring everything almost to a simmer, turn the heat as low as it will go, cover, and forget about it for an hour and a half.
6. Before serving, fish out the bay and thyme, stir in the peas, and let them cook for five minutes — just until bright.
7. Ladle into bowls with bread on the side. The potatoes will have thickened the broth in their own good way, and the garlic cloves will have turned into soft, mild pockets that the honored guest at your table should be invited to squeeze onto the bread.

Pancake Batter, Memorized Forever

If you learn one recipe by heart, learn this one, because it scales, adapts, and rescues breakfast forever.

The rule is: one, one, one.

1 egg
1 cup of flour
1 cup of milk
plus a pinch of salt and a teaspoon of sugar or none, depending on your politics

Method:
1. Crack the egg into a bowl, add the milk, and whisk to combine.
2. Add the flour and the pinch of salt and whisk until nearly smooth. Pancakes by nature prefer a batter with a few small lumps left in it, so exercise mercy.
3. Let the batter rest for fifteen minutes if you have the patience, which lets the flour fully drink the liquid and makes a more tender result.
4. Heat a pan to medium, wipe it with butter on a paper towel, and wait until a drop of water danced on the surface evaporates on contact.
5. Pour in enough batter for a thin puddle — the pancakes of this recipe are the thin, foldable kind — and tip the pan in a circle to spread it.
6. When the top has lost its shine and the edges begin to lift (about a minute), flip once and give it thirty seconds on the second side.
7. Slide onto a warm plate and repeat, buttering the pan barely every time. The first pancake of every batch belongs to the cook, by ancient agreement.

For a thick batter, double the egg and halve the milk. For crepes for a crowd, triple everything and stack the pancakes under a clean towel; the towel is more important than the butter on Sundays.
