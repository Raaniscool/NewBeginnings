A peninsula is a piece of land that has water on most sides but remains joined to a larger landmass. Florida is a peninsula; so is Italy; so is the rocky thumb of land that ventures into the harbor at the end of our street. The word shares its root with 'insular' and 'isolate', which is fitting, because life on a peninsula is always a compromise: it belongs to the land, lives with the sea, and is defined by the narrow place where the two shake hands.

A fraction and a ratio are cousins that keep being mistaken for each other. A fraction always compares a part with the whole it was cut from — three quarters of a pie is three parts of a pie divided into four. A ratio compares any two quantities at all, related or not: three cups of flour to two cups of sugar, or forty kilometers to one hour. Every fraction is a ratio, but not every ratio is a fraction. If this seems like pedantry, consider the baker who doubles a recipe: she multiplies the flour and sugar by two because the ratio must stay the same even though the fractions of dough have changed utterly.

The perimeter of a field and the area of the same field answer different questions, and confusing them is one of the oldest homework mistakes in the world. The perimeter is the length of the fence you would need to walk around the field — a line, measured in meters. The area is the amount of grass inside the fence — a surface, measured in square meters. A long, thin paddock and a square one can share a perimeter and have wildly different areas, just as two people can walk the same street in steps of very different sizes. When a question asks how much fence, think perimeter; when it asks how much lawn seed, think area.

'Because' and 'so' are twin doors that open opposite directions of the same corridor. 'Because' walks backward from a statement to its cause: the match is lit because there were dry leaves. 'So' walks forward from a cause to its consequence: the leaves were dry, so the match took. Any explanation worth anything can be walked in both directions without tripping. That is, in fact, a practical test of whether an explanation is understood at all: if you can run a chain of reasoning both ways, from cause to effect and from effect back to cause, you have understood it. If you can run it in one direction only, you have memorized it.
===
An apology and an excuse look alike at first hearing, but they are built to opposite ends. An apology names the fault and does not bargain: 'I shouted; I was wrong; I am sorry.' An excuse names the circumstances and trades them for mercy: 'I shouted because the day was long.' Both have their places in civilized life, but they are not interchangeable, and the person who tries to pass an excuse as an apology soon discovers that it has the signature reversed at the destination. The test is silent: does the sentence ask for anything? An apology gives; an excuse requests.
===
'Say' and 'tell' keep company but have different appetites. You say words, but you tell people things — the difference is that 'tell' was invented to carry a person as well as a message. You say what you think; you tell me what you think; and you can say something to me, with 'to' doing the carrying. Between them lies a small but reliable traffic law of English, the breaking of which is one of the first signs of a visitor from another language. The fact that native speakers follow the law without studying it is a quiet reminder that languages are learned twice — once by ear, effortlessly, and once forever in the classroom.
===
A theory and a fact are not rungs on the same ladder, and the guess that they are has pleased generations of pedants and confused two generations of everyone else. A fact is a small, hard, verifiable observation: today's rain wet the pavement. A theory is the larger scaffolding that makes sense of a thousand such facts at once: rain happens because wet air cools and drops its water. Facts can be true without explaining anything; theories explain without ever being finished. To say 'it is only a theory' of gravity is like saying 'she is only a map' of a clockmaker: it mistakes the deepest kind of work for the shallowest.
===
A lake and a pond are distinguished less by size, ultimately, than by their own physics. The distinction most naturalists accept is this: a lake is deep enough that rooted plants cannot grow across the middle, while a pond is shallow enough for light to reach the bottom everywhere. By that simple measuring stick, the grand 'pond' at the manor house turns out to be a lake, and half the little 'lakes' in the hills are ponds. The question of what a thing truly is keeps turning out, in science and life, to be a question about how it behaves rather than what it is called.
===
'Few' and 'a few' differ by exactly one letter and one full mood. 'She has a few friends' is comfortable: some, enough, a small warm handful. 'She has few friends' is nearly a complaint: not many, too few, a cold cupboard. The same trick works for 'little' and 'a little' — a little time is all we need; little time is what we have left. English hides whole sympathies in its smallest words, and the difference between promising and complaining often comes down to the children of one tiny article that nobody reads aloud.
===
Average and typical are used daily as if they were interchangeable, and the difference matters most when the numbers are least friendly. The average is computed by adding and dividing; the typical is whatever occurs most often. The average income in a village with one millionaire and nine fishermen is a number that describes no actual villager, while the typical income is what a fisherman earns. Averages are easily seduced by outliers; typicals are stubborn and unglamorous. When a salesperson quotes 'average', ask what is typical, and watch the conversation change its footing.
===
'Effect' and 'affect' walk directly into each other's profession, and English waits with nets for the unwary. 'Affect' is almost always the verb: the rain affected the harvest; it means 'influenced'. 'Effect' is almost always the noun: the effect of the rain was a poor harvest; it means 'the result'. There are rare crossings — you can 'effect a change', meaning 'cause' — but daily use is kind to the simple rule: if it is something you do, spell it with an a; if it is something that results, spell it with an e. The words even know their own hints: action with a, end result with e.
===
Invention and discovery are often praised with the same breath, but the difference between them defines the shape of thanks we owe. Discovery happens when a traveler finds what was always there and waiting: gold in a stream, penicillin in a dish, a star in the telescope. Invention happens when a maker arranges old things into something that has never been: a wheel, a steam engine, a method of vaccination. Fire was discovered; cooking was invented. The electron was discovered; the telephone was invented. The world changes by both, and it is worth knowing which genius you are being asked to thank.
===
'Heard' and 'listened' do not mean the same thing in any language that takes them seriously. We hear with our ears automatically, like windows with no shutters: the rain on the roof, the gulls at the harbor, the engine outside that refuses to start. We listen with our attention deliberately: we lean toward a voice, interpret a hesitation, give a melody its second hearing and find the gifts it hid the first time. Anyone with working ears can hear; anyone with working attention can listen; and the rarest people you will ever meet are those who can do the second thing consistently, at the same time as the first.
===
A rule and a habit dress alike and are not related. A rule is shared and enforced from outside: a library's quiet hours, a law against parking on the corner. A habit is private and enforced from inside: brushing your teeth, water for the cat before your own tea. Rules fail the moment no one is watching; habits are exactly what we do when no one is watching. One sign of a well-ordered group is that its true rules are few and its good habits many, because a society governed entirely by rules needs police on every corner, while a society governed by habit can leave the door unlocked.
===
'Practice' and 'rehearsal' sound interchangeable until you need both at once. Practice is the repeated doing of a thing to make it a permanent ability: scales at the piano, free kicks at the goal, spelling at the kitchen table. Rehearsal is the specific preparation of a specific performance: the wedding toast before the wedding, the concert program before the concert. You practice the piano for years; you rehearse the sonata for the recital. The musician who only rehearses never grows; the musician who only practices never performs; the good ones know when they have crossed from one room into the other.
===
A question of fact and a question of judgment wear each other's overcoats into argument after argument. 'Did the train arrive late?' is a question of fact, answered by a clock. 'Was the delay handled well?' is a question of judgment, answered by everything else. Most quarrels dissolve the moment the two kinds are separated, because people can agree about facts and disagree honorably about judgment, and cannot do the reverse. The best discussion habit is also the simplest: name which kind of question you are answering before you answer it.
