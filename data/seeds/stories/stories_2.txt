When old Meister Braun died, the town found a brass key in his desk with a paper tag that read: "For the smallest desk. The rent is paid until April." Nobody could imagine what he meant. Braun had been the stationer for forty-two years, and he had sold everyone their exercise books, their wedding announcements, and their grief stationery, always saying the same quiet thing: "Paper remembers what memory forgets, madam, if you give it the respect of two corners pressed."

His desk in the back of the shop was enormous — oak, three tiers of drawers, the kind of desk that forces a man to develop opinions. The smallest of those drawers had a keyhole that matched the brass key, and the notary, the pastor, and the three Mrs. Güldens of the parish could not decide which of them had the moral right to look inside. In the end it was Braun's niece Emma, twelve years old and in mourning for the person who showed her how to sharpen a pencil without ever breaking the lead, who went in alone on All Saints' afternoon and opened the drawer.

Inside there was no money. There were letters, tied by year, from almost every family in town. All of them were addressed to Braun in the neat awful hand of the grateful: from the Widow Sommer, thanking him for paper on which she had written her husband's last words down, before forgetfulness; from Herr Kiesel across the street, thanking him for looking the other way on the price of notebooks in 1917; from a clerk of the railway, whose daughter had once written her whole name for the first time in Braun's shop and cried; from the mayor, thanking Braun for the receipt-book advice that had saved the town from a bankruptcy nobody else remembered.

At the bottom, under everything, there was one more envelope marked: "Final inventory."

The inventory listed the contents of the drawer exactly, and at the end Braun had written in the carbon-copy purple of his receipts:

"Rent, as above, until April: the lives one gets to see when one sells paper. — B."

My mother believed in bread the way other people's mothers believed in bank accounts. The sourdough in our house was older than me and my two brothers put together by weight of years, for she had carried a cure of it over the mountain in 1951 in a jam jar wrapped in her wedding shawl, shouting at the Norwegian conductor when he objected to it being alive.

"It is more alive than this train," she said, and the conductor sat down.

Fifty years that starter lived with us, in the blue crock by the window, smelling like small gods and beer, getting fed with rye and opinions. Every Thursday the house smelled of baking for kilometers; people driving east with the windows down could tell, at the curve before our house, that it was Thursday.

My brothers went to America and stayed in hotels. I stayed home and fed the starter. Every year my mother wrote in her notebook the four things that were important about the day's weather, and nothing else:

"1963, May: drought in the oats, happy larches. 1971: mild, mild, everything mild, neighbors contentious. 1979, September: frost so early the plums fell like small letters; S. ill. 1988: fog eight days, wound heals slowly."

On the day after her funeral I got up in the morning with the hangover one always regrets, and the blue crock by the window had gone out. It was flat as a pond at noon, after fifty years of rising. I stood there with the wooden spoon in my hand and the rye open and felt the peculiar terror of being outlived by something that only ever wanted flour and patience.

So I called the widow Fossum, who was the only other person in the valley to whom mother had ever passed a measure of the starter, and she came with a little glass. "Your mother," she said, "said there will be days when the mother does not rise. That day, you go to whoever kept a bit back from last year. Bread," she said, "is not about keeping things. Bread is about passing things on."

We fed the blue crock with the borrowed life, and the wooden spoon is now mine. It is still Thursday. Drivers still open their windows at the curve, though the telephone pole that carried our number in 1988 has rotted away, and if the starter knows who is holding the spoon, it makes no sign.

For three summers, the village of Kappelbach had a well that did not obey. The well was the property of nobody, which in the canton meant it was the responsibility of everybody, and the well, being old and made of stone, apparently had not been consulted.

In midsummer it ran dry — the first time in living memory that anyone had stood on the lip looking into a darkness that did not contain water. That first summer, the families with the newest houses blamed the families with the oldest houses, because an old house with a wet cellar is a house that steals.

The second summer, when the well ran dry again in July, the oldest houses blamed the newest, because the well-digger who made the new foundations had broken the underground aquifer with his digger, and everyone had seen him do it.

The third summer, the mayor ordered an expert. The expert arrived with three bicycles' worth of apparatus, sampled the earth for a fortnight, and announced that the aquifer ran along the clay shelf and entered Kappelbach's bed from uphill, beneath a meadow belonging to Frau Winkler, who could be persuaded for seven hundred francs to have her meadow investigated. Frau Winkler, who had been married to a well-digger, could not be persuaded for seven hundred francs. She consented for nothing, and the investigation came up with two observations; one, the meadow absorbed the water completely before it could settle into the village well — a result of principles — and, two, Frau Winkler had been watering the meadow every single warm night for three summers.

When she was accused, politely, at the assembly, Frau Winkler said: "An old woman is permitted to be careful with her meadow."

"Frau Winkler," the mayor said, "the meadow drinks the water of the whole village."

Frau Winkler stood up. "The meadow of the whole village," she said, "is being looked after by an old woman."

For a moment nobody said anything. Then, without motion, the assembly voted in favor of four hundred meters of new gutter pipe, paid for by everybody, to carry the summer's surplus past the well directly into the Winkler meadow in exchange for two taps on a standpipe in her fence, and Frau Winkler brought her own folding chair to the mayor's office every Tuesday after it was done, to make sure he was sticking to the arrangement.

In a house in Dublin where I called for repairs on a Tuesday, there was a biscuit tin, blue and rusted, that the grandmother of the family asked me to carry down from the highest wardrobe, while she sat in the armchair and pulled a sweater so far over her fingers that only her eyes were visible.

"I never married Keith, you know," she said, "but his photograph is in that tin, and I am eighty-three, and I have decided to look at it." She pointed at the wardrobe. "If I die before you bring it down, dear, tell my granddaughter the truth: that I was brave enough, today, only I was slow."

I brought down the tin. She opened it as if there were steam inside, took out a straw envelope, and laid the photograph on the armchair cover. Keith, nineteen, was smiling in white flannels in 1961, ready for tennis, beside a court that no longer existed. He looked paper-thin, in the way that all paper-thin people in black-and-white photographs look as if they forgive you for having forgotten to know them.

"Keith," the grandmother said solemnly, "this is the man who fixes people's shelves."

I am not a sentimental man, but I stayed for tea. On the inside of the tin's lid there was an inscription in blue biro, in the rush handwriting of a schoolgirl: "If you are reading this, it means you were happy."

"I'm still deciding," the grandmother said.
