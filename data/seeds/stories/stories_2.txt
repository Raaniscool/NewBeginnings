The Brass Key

Nina found the brass key under a floorboard in the spare room, the morning they began clearing out the old house. It was a heavy key, older than the house itself perhaps, with a bow shaped like a four-leaf clover, and it lay in the dust as if it had been waiting with great patience.

Her father identified it at once. "Your grandmother's writing desk," he said. "She locked it every night and made such a ceremony of the key that I thought it was treasure. It opens the bottom drawer. The desk is in the shed."

The shed was full of broken chairs, paint cans, and the smell of rain. At the back, under a blanket stiff with dust, stood a small writing desk of dark wood, its surface scarred by a century of elbows and ink. Nina tried the key. It turned with a sound like a small door closing elsewhere in the world.

Inside the bottom drawer was a tin box of letters, tied with a ribbon gone gray, and a photograph of a young woman in a dance hall. The young woman was unmistakably her grandmother — the same impatient mouth, the same chin — laughing at the camera, her hair full of flowers.

Nina read the letters over three evenings, head bent by the window. They were written by a boy who left for the war and came back quiet, by a friend who moved to Canada and wrote about snow, by her grandmother's sister, who scolded in four languages. There were recipes marked 'try this,' and one page, folded twice, that her grandmother had written to herself at nineteen. It read: 'Things I intend to do: learn to drive the farm lorry. See the sea at least once. Dance in a hall with mirrors. Keep every letter.'

On Sunday Nina carried the tin box to the cemetery with a small bunch of lilac, and stood there not knowing what to say. "Your list," she said at last, and her voice surprised her. "You did all of it. I checked. Dad says you drove the lorry better than Grandpa."

She went home by the long road, past the closed dance hall with its rusty sign, and for no reason she could name, she set the flowers from her own hair — she had worn them for the walk, the way grandmothers and girls in bricks-and-paint towns sometimes both do — carefully at the base of its door.

Sourdough

Everyone agreed that Aldo's bread was the best in three villages, and everyone was mystified by why, because Aldo was cheerfully incapable of keeping a secret about anything else. The flour came from the same mill that supplied everyone. The oven was old and temperamental, like all the village ovens. The salt was salt.

His son Tommaso asked him one winter morning, while they watched the dough rise beside the stove, whether there was a trick to it.

"There is," said Aldo, "but you will be disappointed."

"Try me."

Aldo reached into the flour crock and brought out a jar no bigger than a fist. Inside was something the color of oatmeal, and it smelled faintly sharp and alive. "This is the trick. The starter. Your grandmother began it the year we married. Every batch of bread takes a spoonful of yesterday's, and leaves a spoonful for tomorrow." He set the jar on the table as if it were a photograph. "That jar has fed every loaf we ever baked your mother, every loaf she ever baked for you."

Tommaso considered this. "So the bread is good because it's old?"

"The bread is good because something alive has been looked after every single day for thirty years," said Aldo. "There are no shortcuts for that, my son. That is why the mill can't sell it to you, and the oven can't bake it for you, and I can't leave it to you in a will. You can only be given it warm, and keep it alive, and one day give it away warm to somebody else."

Tommaso carried a jarful to the city when he moved for work, in a cardboard box marked THIS SIDE UP in his father's handwriting. Neighbors who knew nothing about the village came to rely on Tommaso's bread. And any of them could tell you, if you asked, that it tasted of something that had been started a long time before any of them were born.

The Tenth Winter

The old well at the bottom of Haskin's field had been dry for ten winters when the surveyor's truck came up the lane. The village needed a new water source, the council said, and Haskin's lower field, which hid the old well, was being talked about again for the first time in years.

The well had drowned a boy once, in Haskin's grandfather's time, and the family had let it silt up rather than deepen it. Haskin stood by as the surveyor's men cleared the mouth and lowered their lights, and he answered their questions with the silence of a man reading off an old price.

"The shaft's sound," the foreman reported. "Water quality is good. There's flow down there, Haskin. You'd be doing the village a service."

"I know," said Haskin.

That night he walked down to the well with a lantern and sat on the coping stones he had helped his own father lay. His father had said of the drowned boy only one thing, every time the well came up: "The water never did it. The dark did." Haskin sat there until the cold got into his knees, and in the end he did what men in families do, which is to argue quietly with men who are no longer alive to lose the argument.

In the spring, the village cut a proper path to the well and fenced it with chestnut rails that children could see through but not fall over. Haskin's grandson planted the line of white stones that led to it, four summers old and very serious about the spacing. The water was sweet then as it had ever been.

Families keep their griefs the way fields keep their stones: you work around them for a while, and then one year you lift them and build something with them that people need.

The Photograph

The box of photographs lived on the top shelf of Aunt Sera's closet, and it had only one rule: you had to be very careful with the glass ones. The year Anna turned twelve, she was finally allowed, one August afternoon, to sit on the bedroom floor and go through the box, glass plates first, while Aunt Sera mended a hem and answered questions.

The plates were hard to read until you held them to the window, and then the whole careful world of them appeared: a man with a bicycle, two women with hats like upturned boats, a dog that looked exactly like Sera's dog. Anna said so, and Sera laughed. "It's the same dog," she said. "All our dogs have been the same dog since before I was born. When the old one gets on in years, the shepherd brings us a pup marked the same, and we give it the same name. It's cheaper than grieving."

Anna looked at her aunt to see if she was serious, and found that she was and was not at the same time.

At the bottom of the box was a portrait of a young man Anna had never seen, in a uniform unfamiliar to her, with eyes the color of the china in the front room. He looked kind and a little lost, as young men do in portraits meant to be remembered by. The name on the back was Sera's mother's maiden name, and a date, and a single word: Tomas.

"Your great-uncle," Sera said, without looking up from her stitch. "My mother's brother. He went to sea to be a photographer, and the sea kept him before he could send any pictures home. That picture was taken in the fortnight before he left, by his friend the chemist, as a joke."

"A joke?"

"Photographers don't like to be photographed, Anna. They like to hold the machine. Tomas said a man behind a camera is never lost." She set the stitch and snipped the thread with her teeth. "When you go out into the world — and you will go, I've watched you at the window — you send your mother pictures. Not of the sights. Of your face. That's the difference between living somewhere and being lost there."

Anna kept the glass plate of Tomas on her desk for the rest of the year. When she left for the university, her mother found, taped by string inside every parcel of biscuits and woolens, a photograph of Anna: at her desk with the plate propped behind her, on the steps of the library, under the chestnut tree, never of the sights, always of her face.
