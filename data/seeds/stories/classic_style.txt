The Wind and the Oak

A great Oak grew upon a hill, and prided himself upon his strength. "See how the years have honored me," said he, "and how the storms have tried me and been refused."

A slender Reed grew at the foot of the hill, beside a lazy brook, and the Oak looked down upon her with pity. "Little cousin," said he, "how do you live, so weak as you are, in a world that is all wind?"

"I bend," said the Reed, "and so I stand."

The Oak laughed, for the word stood against everything he knew of standing.

But that same winter came the great gale, the gale of which the oldest men of the village still speak when the chimneys smoke strangely. All night it leaned upon the hill with its whole weight. The Oak stood as he had always stood, and when the first light came, the people found him as they had never seen him: uprooted, all his strength at last the very thing that had brought him down, for he could not bend.

They say the Reed danced all night and scarcely noticed, being busy being a reed.

Strength is a wonderful thing, until it is asked to do the one thing it cannot.

The Miller and the River

There was once a miller who owned a mill upon a river, and he was forever at war with the river. In spring it flooded his wheel; in summer it left him dry; in winter it froze his stones to silence. "Ungrateful water!" he would cry, shaking his floury fist at the stream. "Have I not given you a wheel to turn and a name to be known by, and is this how you thank me?"

One dry summer, when the river was a thread and the millpond a bed of cracked clay, the miller walked upstream in a great rage to curse the river at its source, as he imagined it. He walked three days and found, instead of a source, a thousand little springs, each no bigger than his thumb, each giving what little it had.

And he understood that he had been wrong his whole life, though not in the way he had supposed. He had thought the river a debtor who paid reluctantly; but the river had never promised him anything at all. It simply gave what the season gave, and the season answers to no one.

He went home and built a second wheel, smaller and higher, for the dry months, and a sluice for the wet ones, and though he grumbled as much as ever — for grumbling was the greater part of his constitution — the village noticed that the bread was never again late.

It is a poor bargain to fight what will not negotiate, and a rich one to learn its habits.

The Princess and the True Question

In a kingdom whose name is forgotten, the king proclaimed that whoever could answer the princess's one true question should marry her and inherit the crown. This was, as everyone agreed, a puzzling decree, for the princess had never asked any question in public, and those who knew her best said she rarely asked them in private either.

Princes came with answers beyond counting. One brought the answer to the riddle of the Sphinx; another, the true name of the west wind; a third had memorized the whole tax roll of his father's kingdom and recited it admirably. The princess heard them all with the same polite attention, and dismissed them all with the same polite regret.

At last a young gardener came, with earth still on his boots, for he had not expected to be let in and had scarcely combed his hair. The princess asked him, as she asked everyone, what he had brought.

"Nothing, Your Highness," he said. "Only a question of my own, which is this: what is your true question? For I have been among the roses all my life, and roses never tell you theirs — you have to learn to ask."

The princess laughed for the first time in that long year of suitors, and the courtiers, who understood nothing of gardens, understood at once that the contest was over.

"That," said the king, who was not without wisdom, "is what I have been waiting for someone to notice. My daughter does not want answers; she has a library full of answers. She wants to be asked."

Whether the gardener made a good king the histories do not record, for the histories were written by the courtiers. But it is noticed that the royal gardens flourished exceedingly in his reign, and that the queen was often seen in them, asking questions.

The House in Lantern Lane

In the town where I spent my boyhood there stood a house in Lantern Lane that every child knew to be haunted, though no living child could produce a single witness to the ghost. We knew it the way we knew that the baker's dog could understand arithmetic: it was among those things too generally believed to require evidence.

The house had once belonged to old Mr. Alva, the lamplighter, who in the days before the wires came had walked the lane each dusk with his long pole, waking the lamps one by one like a man waking children, gently and in the same order every time. He had no family that anyone knew, and when he died — quietly, in his chair, with his boots polished — the house remained much as he had left it, and the lamps, which were electric now, remained lit.

The truth, which I learned considerably later and corroborated with the town clerk, was less marvelous and more moving than our ghost. In his will, old Alva had left a sum of money with a single provision: that the lamp before his door should be kept lit every night without exception, and that on wet nights somebody — it did not matter who — should be paid to wipe its glass, 'for a lane with one dark lamp is a dark lane.'

We children, passing on winter evenings, felt the comfort of that lamp without knowing its name, and mistook comfort for haunting, as the young so often do. The house was pulled down at last to make room for the new road, but the lamp was saved, and it stands today outside the clerk's office, where it is, I am told, scrupulously cleaned on wet nights.

I do not know whether Mr. Alva believed in ghosts. I believe, on the evidence, that he believed in lamps.

The Cobbler's Apprentice

It is said that a cobbler of great reputation once took on an apprentice who was, by universal account, the worst apprentice in the recorded history of shoemaking. His stitching wandered like a drunkard; his leather was cut with the certainty of a man guessing; and his lasts squeaked in protest. The cobbler, a patient man, bore it for three years.

In the fourth year a traveler came through the town with boots so fine that the cobbler was called out of his shop to admire them. He examined the stitching and grew quiet. He examined the sole and grew quieter.

"Who made these?" he asked at last.

"A man in my village," the traveler said. "A strange fellow. He wears a cobbler's apron and keeps a cobbler's hours, and when he is not making boots he is walking in them — forty, fifty miles at a stretch, to see where they fail."

The cobbler sent for his apprentice that evening and set the boots between them on the bench. "Yours?" he asked.

The apprentice looked at the floor. "I hid the walking from you, master, because you told me an apprentice's place is at the bench. But the bench teaches the shoe, and the road teaches the boot, and I wanted to learn boots."

The old cobbler sat for a long time with the boots in his lap. Then he did a thing that cost him more than the three wasted years: he admitted, aloud and before his tools, that his bench had taught him only shoes, and that at sixty years of age he had never once walked forty miles to discover how his work held up in the world it was made for.

The two of them were seen, in the years that followed, walking the long roads together in every season, master and apprentice in the same boots at last.

A skill is not finished at the bench. It is finished where it is used.

The Bird Seller

In the market of a certain city there worked a seller of birds whose shop was more crowded than any other, and the reason was a puzzle to the neighbors, for his birds were no brighter, no cheaper, and no better singers than the birds of the other shops. There was, however, posted upon his door a sign, hand-lettered and much faded, which read: NO BIRD SOLD TO A CLOSED ROOM.

Nobody enforced the sign, and the other sellers mocked it as madness — what merchant refuses silver? But here is the strange part: his customers lined up. They came, they said later, because a man who worried about the room would worry about the bird, and a man who worried about the bird would worry about the seed he sold with it, and the cage, and the advice after the sale. And they were right, for he did worry about all those things, and people sense the difference between a merchant and a merchant.

He died old and never rich, and the other sellers inherited his trade. The sign, worn beyond saving, was thrown out with his effects. But it is a fact of that city's quiet history that within a year the second shop had repainted it, and within three years the whole market had adopted it, and the custom persists there to this day.

An honest rule written on a door travels further than a clever one in a ledger.
