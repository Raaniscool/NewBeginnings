A Wind and an Oak were contending, as they had contended since time out of mind, which of them had served the World best. The Wind was young and full of argument; the Oak was old and had ceased to argue with anything, but the Wind mistook the silence for weakness.

"I feed the Sailor," said the Wind, "and carry the Rain to every dry Field. Without me, your Acorns would lie in your Shadow forever."

"True," said the Oak, "and I receive your Acorns back in good earth, that their Grandchildren may stand as I do. I do not carry your Rain; but then, I do not carry it away."

"Old Sovereign," boasted the Wind, "you live in one Place. The World runs where I run; I have shaken the Masts of Ships in Ten Nations before breakfast."

"And blown away," said the Oak, "with equal Residence in None. You may move, my Friend, as you please; but as for me, I prefer to remain where my Children can find me."

Now it chanced that a great Storm came up from the West, and the Wind, ambitious to demonstrate its Superiority, joined himself to the Storm and came against the Oak with all his Strength. He pulled at the Oak by every leaf until the great Trunk groaned like a Ship at Anchor. But the Oak, having roots that remembered things the Wind had never known, inclined and did not prove; and when the Storm had spent itself, the Parson's Field behind was blown bare, but the Oak stood, and the Acorns fell in their own good place.

So it is with those who serve by constancy: they are little praised while they stand, but every Storm for an hundred Years has called the Wind great, and yet the Wind has never once been a Field.

There was a Miller whose Mill stood upon the River, where it had stood for four generations; and he had a Son whom he wished to leave it to. When the Boy was grown, the Miller called him and said: "Tomorrow the River is mine; after me, yours."

The Son, who loved Arithmetic and had read much besides, answered: "Father, is the River ours then?"

"As near as Water can be, it is," said the Miller. "We pay the Toll, we grind where the Lord lets us grind."

"Then I do not want it," said the Son, "for nothing that cannot be held should be owned."

The Miller was silent a long Time. Then he took his Son by the Shoulder and led him to the Mill-Race, where the Water turned her wheel Day and Night in the same honest Gray. "Look then," said he, "and tell me which two Days you have ever seen the same River at this Board. It is His, and it is Ours, as the Great Wheel turns that we fasten nothing to. Your Mother ground wheat here when you cried in the Cradle; the Water carried her Voice to the Sea and changed her not."

It is said the Son stood beside the River for a Afternoon, and did not answer, but on the third Day he tied on the Miller's Apron, picked up the heavy Staff of the fourth Generation, and put his Hand into the Stream as if in Ceremony, blessing it the way his Father had blessed it.

"The River is gone from your Grasp," said the Miller, "before you can count Three."

"Before you can count One," said the Son. "Father, I think I shall stay."

There was once a great and terrible King, who was advised by his Court that every Land required his Hand upon it; and so he made War upon the Earth. In his Youth he conquered the Young Kingdoms, and in his Middle Age the Considerable Ones, and in his Age the Remaining Smaller States; until at last he was master of every Field and Harbor as far as his Ships could reach.

Then he summoned his Chronicler and commanded: "Tell me, as I can see nothing new, what is there left to conquer."

The Chronicler, who was an old and true man, having brought along a Map, laid it upon the King's Table and named every Plain and Island by Name; for the King had conquered them, all, except one, which was framed in Gold at the edge of the Map. It was called, in the manner of the Old Engravers, TOMORROW.

"I shall conquer Tomorrow by Dinner," said the King, and he called for his Armor.

The Chronicler said nothing; war counselors and blacksmiths came instead. By the tenth morning of the expedition, the Chronicler was called again, for the King had found the Gates of Tomorrow firmly unlocked, yet no Territory there. It contained, as far as his Thousands could see, only Sunlight on Empty Fields.

"I have ridden for ten days in an empty Province," said the King, "and spent a Treasury on Armor. Make sense of this."

"If I may, Majesty," said the Chronicler, "everything you have conquered already, the Gold has lost its Shine and the Fortress fallen to Diligence. You do not hold Tomorrow; Nobody does. But no Kingdom conquests are safe for that very reason."

"Then how," said the King, "shall I keep what I have?"

"By Planting," said the Chronicler, "what you cannot reap; for the Field you have fed with your own two Hands will not betray you for the next Kingdom's sake."

The King rode back slowly, and thought many hours upon the Borders of his Great Territories, and those who watched him from the Walls of Conquered Cities thought him Changed. After which he did no new Deed of Arms for the remainder of his days; and he died, it is said, content in a Garden, which is the way that all Maps end, if they are true.

Being an Extract from the Journal of the Late Mr. Thos. Harwell, Lamplighter of this Parish.
Oct. 12. The Town Council having decided that Lantern Lane shall not be lit until the Moon is both Up and Overtaken, I find myself idle at Six, and at my Wits End by Eight; waited at the Corner opposite the Coal Merchant, who came out and asked me, with a strange Earnestness, how soon I would raise the Lights. Sir, said I, I am your most obedient, but I have pledged by the Agreement of my Office not to raise them before the Moon. Whereupon he swore a truly Imperial Oath and removed his Wood into the yard with a lantern of his own.

Oct. 13. Much Activity among the Dogs. About Midnight, having dozed by the third Lantern, I observed Mrs. Beddoes crossing with a great kettle, upon which she had set a candle inside for seeing the Drain, which is flooded; and by its Light remarked that the Moon rose full, impertinently, over the Church. I have raised Lanterns Nos. 3 through 14, and the Shopkeeper salutes me as if it were a Private Gift. Waste, no doubt.

Oct. 14. The Butcher's Boy remarks my Lantern no. 5 is alight while no. 4 is out, and asks if the Council have made a Privy Arrangement with the Dark; I Replied Nothing, for there is no pleasantry for a man in my Station, only Company.

Oct. 16. The Moon is later again. Lantern Lane lies between two Hills, and without lights the Children cannot find their way home from the Quaker School. Mrs. Abbot called down her Window, "Mr. Harwell, you're not Ill, I hope?" — whereupon I lighted Nos. 1 and 2 by private hesitation, which is Disorder, and yet, if any Parent of the Parish sleeps tonight in more Confidence than by reason of my two Matches, I shall not grieve.

So here I end this Humble Narrative; the Council may vote as they please on Sunrise, but I shall Proclaim with my Lanterns every Evening what any Apprentice could tell them, that a Parish is not governed by the Sky, but by whoever brings Light to Others at the Hour of Its Need.

The village of Wissing, in the County, was remarkable in my Youth for one thing above all, which was that nobody in it locked a Door. It was a Prosperity of the working Good, the sort that makes Life plain and the Bread-Line short, which is called Safety by the Town Merchant and Faith by the Pastor; the Village was safe, it was said, because of the Good Brown Door of Mr. Sibberley the Shoemaker.

Mr. Sibberley was not a Shoemaker exactly; he was the son of one, and he had been a Sailor, and a Hatter, and a great many things besides; but he had come home and taken up the Shop when he was thirty, and there he stayed behind by that Great Brown Door which was kept painted in a New Coat each Michaelmas. The door was the wonder of Wissing: it had been painted fifteen years, and Mr. Sibberley kept the Key (for my Father, who was the Sexton, told me there was one, never used) hung on the inside nail just behind the Corners of a Map of Cornwall he had bought in Bath and valued nothing at.

When the War came, and Wissing was not spared, the Great Brown Door of Mr. Sibberley never locked the first three Days; on the Fourth, when the worst news reached our Market, I observed him take down the Key from the Nail, and put it in his Pocket, and walk from house to house turning Other People's Locks with a Great Tenderness, telling the Children with a Grinning Certainty that the fatherly Map of Cornwall had vouched for the Village's safety, and that all Locked Doors are but Shoes for Humility to keep the Rain off.

When the War ended, Mr. Sibberley did not touch the brown paint any more; that Year there was no Michaelmas. He was buried in the Churchyard with the Key in his pocket, and the Door of his Shop stands open to this day. My Father, who was a Religious Man, said the Secret of Wissing was that the Village had always known a Door is only ever a Door in the world, while a Man is sometimes his House; and being a simple Man, I add only this: we walk in and out of doors in Wissing as if Life were Intimate still, and Sibberley's Shop stands Smiling by the Map, welcoming the way neighbors.

When the first Hint of Winter tried the Windows of the great City, there was an Ordinary Occurrence at the Corner of Pantry Lane: a Person, very Seedy and Blue, entered the Shop of Mr. Custance, the Bird-Seller, and stood looking upon the Warming Canaries for a full Quarter-hour.

Mr. Custance was Trading in Canaries since before the Passing of the Smoke Nuisance Bills, and had a Sharp Eye for a Customer. "Are you in want of Bird, sir?" asked he, after the Fashion of the Trade.

The Person did not speak, but Signed at the third Cage from the Front, in which a particularly Ordinary Brown Canary was making itself comfortable among the Perches, as if to indicate with all his Fingers what he Wished to See proved: is it warm enough? Mr. Custance reached for his Wool Glove, which was on the Nail, and opened the Cage.

The Canary made an Elegant Bow, and attempted to Fly, and landed on the Visitor's Woollen Cap, as if it were the Cap's very own Property; sang Two Warbles in a Thoughtful Manner; and then kept quite Still. The Visitor began to weep— no other Word suffices, no more dignity than that— and there in the Shop of Mr. Custance, in the cold Hour of the first Winter Snow, he stood, and wept.

It came out that the Visitor had come to the City from a Village where his Father had kept a Singing Bird; had sold it, twenty-five years before, on the Morning of June 12th, in the reign of Amalgamated Bird-Merchants, calling at Tuesday-Last; he was now Homeless, having followed two Trade Routes into Failure and the Threshing-Machine Business in Baker Street; and as he stood there with His Bird on His Cap, the little Ordinary Creature seemed to remember all of its two Ounces of history, and settled its Feathers, and was unafraid.

Mr. Custance accepted no Money. "Your Bird, sir," said he, "I know the Cage, though the Bird is different. A Smile from the Song to the Singer— that is the best of Trades done."

It was two Years later before the Bird-Seller learned the Visitor had become the Keeper of the Night Shelters of this City, and required every New Year's Budget brought before the Corporation to specify a Cage Item. The Budget was always Voted Down. Every Year he sent the Corporation the same Note: "A Bird that stays where she is Warm is no Burden; the Cages, gentlemen, are what should Cold remain."
