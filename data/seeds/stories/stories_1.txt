The Lighthouse and the Letter

For twelve years, Anton had kept the lighthouse on Gannett Rock alone. His world was a small tower, a lamp he lit at dusk, and the sea, which changed its mood daily and never its mind. Twice a month a boat came with supplies and letters, and Anton saved the letters for last, the way some men save dessert.

In October, a letter came from a lawyer in the city. Anton's sister had died in August, the letter said, and had left him her cottage and everything in it. There was also a girl. His niece. Twelve years old, with no other family, and the law wished to know whether the lighthouse keeper intended to take her, or whether the state should.

Anton read the letter standing at the kitchen table. Then he climbed the tower, lit the lamp as he always did, and sat down to think while the beam went round and round over the black water. He had not seen the child since she was small enough to trip over her own boots. A lighthouse, he knew, was no place for a girl. There was the school to think of, and friends, and dances she would one day want, and all the things a man with oil on his hands knew nothing about.

But there was also the empty chair at his table, which had been empty a long time.

When the supply boat came again, the captain found Anton waiting on the jetty with his bag already packed. "Just for the winter," Anton said, as if the sea were listening. "Somebody should tell the girl what her mother was like."

The lamp was lit by a keeper hired from the mainland that winter. But neighbors in the village near the school noticed something worth a story: a tall, weather-cured man who arrived by bus every Friday and waited at the gate with his hat in his hands, and a thin girl who walked faster when she saw him, every single Friday, from October until the green returned to the fields.

Marta's Fish

Marta ran the fish stall in the market, as her mother had before her, and she knew two things about every customer: what they wanted and what they would accept instead. Old Simons came every Friday for herring, and if there was no herring he stood there, hat in hand, unable to imagine a different dinner. So Marta always kept herring for Simons, even when herring was dear.

One Friday in Lent the herring did not come. The boats had stayed in harbor for the storm, and the market smelled of nothing but wet canvas. Marta watched Simons approach across the square, steady as a clock, and she did a thing she had never done. She wrapped two fat mackerel in herring paper.

"Good morning, Mr. Simons," she said. "There you are."

"God bless you, Marta," said Simons, and paid, and went home with his package under his arm.

All week Marta worried. Old men and their dinners are not to be trifled with. But on the next Friday Simons came at his usual time, and there was something new in his face, a kind of brightness.

"Marta," he said, "for fifty years I have eaten herring on Fridays, because on Fridays a man eats herring. Last week my Anna cooked what you sold me, and we agreed it was the finest fish we ever ate. Was that really herring?"

Marta confessed that it was not.

"Then sell me more of whatever herring it wasn't," said Simons, and he laughed so loudly that two pigeons fled the rafters, and that year Marta hung a small new sign over the stall, at Anna's suggestion: FISH FOR FRIDAYS, ALL KINDS WELCOME.

The Night Bus

The last bus out of the valley ran at eleven forty, and on most nights it carried nobody but its driver, a patient woman named Oksana who hummed while she drove. One December night the bus was full. First there was a boy with a cardboard box, which he kept on his knees and steadied at every turn. Then a nurse still in her uniform, asleep before the second stop. Then an old man carrying a cello case nearly as tall as himself. Then two young sisters sharing a single pair of gloves, one glove each.

At Mill Cross a man got on soaked to the bone, because the rain that night had no mercy. He counted his coins twice and was still short, and he stood there in the aisle dripping and apologizing to Oksana with his shoulders as much as his words.

"I've got it," said the boy with the box, and put his fare and the stranger's fare into the tray together, coin by counted coin.

The bus rolled on into the wet dark. The nurse woke and, seeing the soaked man shivering, gave him her scarf. The sisters moved up so he could sit near the heater. The old man opened his cello case and handed out, of all things, oranges — six of them, which he said were for his students, though there were exactly enough for everyone except himself.

Oksana drove the rest of the route with nine oranges on the dashboard and, for the first time in eleven years of empty night buses, had humming accompaniment. The cello, it turned out, traveled easier out of its case, and a cello in the rain-dark, with a boy singing to keep his box calm — inside the box was a one-eyed cat, it was later established — is a memory people keep.

The boy never did give his name. But the man who had been short of coins paid his debt forward for years afterward, as the owner of the valley's smallest café, in whose window a small hand-painted sign read: IF YOU ARE SHORT TODAY, TELL US FRANKLY. WE REMEMBER THE NIGHT BUS.

The Field That Flooded

Everyone in the valley blamed the rain when the meadow flooded, everyone except Grandmother Ilsa. "Rain fills a river," she said, "but a river does not climb a hill." So while the village council wrote letters about rain barrels and drainage ditches, Ilsa put on her tall boots and walked upstream.

Three kilometers up, she found the answer doing what it does best: cutting wood. A beaver family had dammed the shallow creek above the mill pond, beautifully, thoroughly, and entirely without permission from the council. The pond's overflow ran straight into the meadow.

At the village meeting, opinions arrived like bad weather. "Trap them," said the miller, whose shed sat lowest. "The law allows it." "Move them miles away," said the schoolteacher, who was fond of beavers in principle and extremely unhappy with them in the meadow. The meeting voted four to three for traps, and Ilsa, who had no vote anymore at her age, went home angry.

Next morning she rose before the baker and walked the long road to the miller's shed. She knocked until he came down, blinking, in his undershirt. "I will pay for the fence myself," she said. "Wire around the base of your shed and along the lowest bank. It is what my father did when the beavers came in the sixties. It is old work, and it holds."

"The traps are decided, Ilsa," the miller said, gently.

"Decisions can be undecided. Show me a trap that catches a flood."

By noon the two of them were unrolling wire along the bank, and by the third day three neighbors were helping, because work attracts workers more surely than talk attracts talkers. The beavers kept their dam, the meadow slowly drained, and the miller — who told the story better than anyone, and told it often, standing dry inside his shed — always ended it the same way: "The law allows many things. A fence allows more."
