The lighthouse at Cape Herron had been automated for eleven years when the letter arrived, but Oswald Brandt still lived in the keeper's cottage because his agreement with the Maritime Authority had never been formally ended, and Oswald was a formal man. He had wound the old clockwork rotation machinery every day for thirty years, long after authority stopped paying for wicks, and when the lamp was switched to electricity he wound it still, saving his own honor the way other men save string.

The letter came on a Thursday, in an envelope so thin it rattled. His daughter Alma wrote to say that she was coming home at the end of August, "with a person I want you to meet, Papa, unless you have forgotten how to receive guests, in which case please do not read the rest of this letter."

Oswald read it for a week.

He had opinions about persons wanting to be met. Persons wanting to be met usually wanted inspecting, and he had been a keeper; his whole profession, when you reduced it to a shape, was telling ships: here is a rock, and here you may not come. Persons were a different category of ship.

On the ninth day he began to make the guest room ready, which had been a storage room for ropes. The cottage was two rooms down and one up, and the storage room was the one up, under the gable, with a round window that looked out at nothing but weather. He threw out nine years of rope coils, catalogued as he went — the twine he kept, thrown into the air like morning hair; the heavy line for hauling boats onto the slip, kept coiled by the door; the old lanyards of his uniform, he put in his bottom drawer, and sat down, because it took him a moment to remember why he had kept them at all.

He wrote back: "Come. Bring the person. Umbrella recommended. — O.B."

He thought it showed openness.
===
For thirteen days the weather was so clean it seemed impertinent, as if the Cape were trying to make a good impression. Oswald cleaned the brass telescope which no ship needed anymore, and wrote in the log: "Visibility wine-clear. Salmon running offshore, gulls prolonged one hour past their usual suppertime." Then he cleaned the telescope again.

In the evening, at the top of the light which no longer needed him, he took out his daughter's last photograph. Alma at twenty, hair cropped like straw. He had not recognized the woman, then, only the wick, burning through. In the past twelve years he had seen his daughter five times. He knew, in a document-style knowledge of the fact, that she was now thirty-two. He had a carpenter's walk around the tower to keep the vertigo honest, and on the second day of August he was up there late, polishing the lamp-room glass which no longer needed polishing, when the radio crackled.

"Kiowa to Cape Herron, Kiowa to Cape Herron, summer picnic ice is five hours late with a party aboard, over."

Oswald looked at the sea, which was trying to look harmless.

"Kiowa, this is Cape Herron." His voice came out rusted at the hinge; his hand knew the old code. "Visibility from the tower is fifteen miles. If your party left late, you are facing the nine o'clock fog bank, which reads gray on the sat-nav but stands like plaster at the waterline. Advise route inside the buoy line. Repeat: inside the buoy line."

There was silence on the radio, and then a laugh. "Cape Herron, I thought nobody lived at that address."

"Mister," Oswald said, "some of us never got the forwarding memo."

The Kiowa thanked him seven times. When she had rounded the headland even the radio seemed warmer. Oswald stayed at the top of the tower and did not, would not, could not look at the mainland where, from the road, a bus carrying his daughter was probably climbing the cliff switchbacks. Nine o'clock fog or no nine o'clock fog, he wanted to be up there with some glass to polish when she arrived.

He found, on the third pass over the pane, that his hands had forgotten the small talk of polish, and were simply holding the rag very tight.
===
The person wanting to be met was a harbor engineer named Theodore, tall as a mast and given to knees. He arrived with a canvas bag of tools ("the roads here rattle every last nut out of a car, Mr. Brandt, so I always travel prepared"), a flat hat like a pancake, and a playlist he kept apologizing for. He had the eyes of a man who assesses load-bearing walls out of habit, and he spent the first ten minutes of the evening assessing Oswald's pantry door.

"That hinge is a disaster waiting to happen, sir," he said, "if you'll forgive me saying so. May I?"

"By all means," Oswald said. "Recklessness is welcome in visitors."

Theodore laughed so hard he dropped the screwdriver. Alma looked from one to the other with the exhaustion of a diplomat, and the evening suddenly flowed like a river that has been sweating against a dam all day. There was fish, stewed; there was bread burnt in patches ("the oven has an opinion," Oswald said); there were candles because the lamp circuit did something irregular on Thursdays. Theodore told the story of a bridge in Crete whose concrete had been poured in the wrong decade, and Oswald found refill in his glass for the fifth time, and Alma smiled mostly at her plate, as if at a drawing she had made as a child and hated and loved.

After supper they played Old Maid, because there were three of them and the cards were the only council the cottage had ever hosted with multiple attendees. Theodore cheated, discovered, and confessed, laughing like a boy. Alma won five hands straight, and leaned back like the queen of an island. At around eleven, fog came in — soft, thoughtful, exactly on time.

Oswald excused himself, walked up the internal stair of the tower, and started the rotations for nothing. The light moved across the water in a fan, indifferent as ever. He thought: this is what they pay me not to do. He thought: by the end of the breakfast, we will have talked about the weather twice. After a while he went down again.

"You sleep up here?" Theodore asked, standing on the stairs with two cups of tea and the terror of a man doing something correctly.

"Used to." Oswald took the cup. "Every night for thirty years. Their light works, mind. It's just me that runs on wound string."

They stood in the fog, in the lamp room, and the fan of light came around and through, and Theodore said, with careful respect: "I want to restore that rotation gear, sir. Alma says it's beautiful. If you'll show me where you keep the oil, I'd like to earn my dinner every day for as long as we're staying."

Oswald thought about it. The wick of the lighthouse that had never gone out in his whole life moved through the night, doing the work of no one, refusing nothing.

"You'll leave fingerprints on everything," he said, which was as close to acceptance as he had ever come, "and you'll drink the bad coffee in the morning under my supervision, Theodore-with-pancake-hat, and maybe you'll tell me about those Crete bridges with concrete poured all wrong. There's oil under the bench."

And he handed the cup of tea back, because his hands had stopped holding things tight, and there was nobody watching but the keeper, and for once the keeper was him.

===
On the third morning of August, Alma found her father in the small hours, reading the old log, at the desk, in the chair of his whole life, with a pencil behind his ear and fourteen registrations of "salmon offshore, prolonged." The guest room door stood open, unroped; Theodore's socks were drying on the second boat hook like a flag, and the station, as far as authority was concerned, was decommissioned, dark, and without need.

But the window looked east, at the empty horizon that had raised him, and the fog coming in was important, he thought, because fog was never the problem; only arriving mattered.

"Drink the bad coffee, Papa," she said, and it was, as ever, an order; and Oswald, as ever, obeyed, and saved himself.

Martina Okoye's fish stall was the seventh from the entrance of the Ashanti Road market, which meant she got the shoppers after they had spent half their courage haggling at the fish stalls one through six, and before they reached Auntie Verona's tomatoes — and no one bought fish after buying Auntie Verona's tomatoes, because the tomatoes were a story in themselves and people wanted to go home and tell it.

So Martina's fish were the fish you bought because you had already decided about dinner, and she knew it, and she priced cheap, but the cheap was honest. Her fish came in on the four o'clock bus from Elmina, packed in crates with the names of crews she knew, and by the time the crates reached her the ice had melted to a dignified discretion and the fish looked as if they had consented.

On the first Wednesday of Lent, Martina found a plastic bag in the crate of snapper, knotted at the top, the kind they gave away at the pharmacy. Inside: a piece of paper folded four times, and money. A lot of money. She did not count it in front of the fish.

The note, in careful schoolboy print: "To whoever finds this. It is the trade from the trawler. I am sorry it came out this way. If you are an honest person, as the fish people are, look after it, and I will come back on Friday before first light to take it and explain. — J."

She read it twice. Then she did nothing for an hour, which is what her mother would have done, and when the sparrows came through the roof slats she flourished her cloth at them, and counted. Six hundred thousand cedis, in rubber bands, smelling of the sea.

Elmina fishermen, Martina knew, did not get paid in rubber bands. This was salary fortune, luck money, bribe money, first-fruit money — whose loss would drown.

On Friday before first light she was at the stall with the plastic bag under the snapper crate, at the hour when even the devout consider the hour's impropriety. A boy of eleven came on a bicycle with no brakes and stopped by scraping his shoe, and saw her, and nearly rode into the gutter.

"Are you honest?" Martina said.

The boy considered her. He was skinny, wet with the morning, and he had eyes like small engines. "Every day, Auntie," he said, "except Wednesday."

"Then tell me how much it is," said Martina, "and don't you dare lie once more, Wednesday expert."

The boy stared at her shoes a long time, and told her the amount to the last cedi. Then he told her about the trawler Forecastle Rose, whose captain owed every crew member three weeks, whose first mate was his cousin Jojo who had traded a week's back pay for his little sister's school fees against the loan-shark. The plastic bag of money was the crew's safe, passed hand to hand from boat to market on the arm of the only person who had no name to be extorted. Jojo was in a cell in Sekondi on a nothing charge, and the shark knew the route, and at eleven years old, Wednesday was the boy's day for being a man.

Martina took the boy's hand and put it in the crate of snapper, cold as a bell. "You look after it," she said, "all day."

By noon the loan-shark came past, twice, wearing a clean shirt and looking for a schoolboy on a breakable bicycle, and found only Martina arranging her snapper with exhausted good humor. At the first fish bus at four, the crew of the Forecastle Rose, all thirty of them in the crew-lorry, collected the bag from under the crate, and Jojo's cousin's hand for a moment shook like a flag in Martina's.

They didn't thank. Crews don't thank, and this is known.

"You keep the Wednesday fish," Martina Okoye said, seventh stall from the entrance, where the shopper has decided about dinner, "and the honest get their dinner anyway."

The number four bus leaves the depot at 23:40, drives the circumference of the whole city, takes forever, and is never on time. Everybody knows this. The woman with the accordion knows it; she plays songs about tomatoes to nobody in particular between the ring road and the dockyards. The boy with the funeral shoes knows it; he rides to the end of the line and back two times before a shift, one leg folded under him like a stroller. The driver, Marina, knows it best of all. She has driven the four for twelve years.

The number four was my bus. I rode it when things had gone wrong: school, then the apprenticeship, then the engagement; and Marina never said a word except the same one, which was: "End of the line, my friend. Everybody out."

Then last February it happened I was in a state that needed every seat of the circumference, all forty minutes of the way. The winter cities went by like slides in a broken projector. Somewhere near the ring road, the accordion woman began "The Girl with the Blue Scarf," and the boy with the funeral shoes hummed the second line by heart.

Marina put her eyes in the mirror. "You, mister," she said. "The one who rides only when it goes wrong."

"Marina," I said, "it has gone so far wrong my mother is presenting. I am presenting at my grandmother's funeral tomorrow. The thing I have to say is in a folder I wrote on the computer of a man who fired me."

The others looked out the windows as if I had the flu. But Marina lifted one palm from the wheel and laid her palm against her own chest.

"Then write it down anew," she said. "With your hand. What is written with the hand goes to the heart of the person listening; what is written on the computer goes to his glass. It is the law of funerals..." she pursed her mouths, "and the law of buses."

So I did. I wrote it on the back of the funeral leaflet at the next traffic light stop, while the accordion woman switched to "Wait For Me, Winter" and the boy with funeral shoes kept my pen from rolling away by holding it down with his elbow.

When we reached the last turnaround, Marina switched off the motor, which she had never once done inside the forty minutes, and listened, elbow propped up. And if I have never told you what I said at my grandmother's funeral, it is because the folder version is long gone, and the leaflet version is in my mother's Bible, and the credits in my bank account of the ex-boss are zero, but the number four bus still leaves the depot at 23:40, drives the circumference, and is never, never on time.

There was once a rich man who had a garden. He had the garden built for him, and employed a gardener to keep it, because a garden, he had heard, is the thing to have; and the gardener built lawns there, planted trees, and kept flowers with a defect to flaunt, everything green and payable. And the rich man was satisfied for a while.

But the garden grew old anyway. Lawns moved, trees dropped, flowers refused to remain in the right bed. It cost more and more, and the rich man thought: what is the use of it? The gardener is rich on the price of the seasons. So the rich man dismissed the gardener, burned the pruning book, and had the whole garden covered with cement, which is very green indeed and requires almost no watering.

For the first few months he entertained there. He stood on the stiff gray of a July afternoon, told his guests that a stone rose never faded, and nobody brought shovels. But little by little the guests thinned, and the rich man was very proud of his garden, and very lonely, and one autumn morning he found a cracked edge of cement where a little green tongue of grass was pushing through.

The rich man called the concrete company to report a defect. The company said: "It is a defect, sir."

"Good," said the rich man. "Then make it better. Pour more."

The company brought its trucks, poured over the grass twice, and charged him extra for the second pour. But the next spring the leaves of three old linden trees came down with green fingers through every cover in the yard, in triangles of dusty morning, and the rich man went out with a chair and watched the ruin of his money.

He sat there for three hours. When he stood up he was crying, which he had not done since the age of forty. He stepped across the little grass tongues and went into the house, and the telephone found the gardener, and the gardener was very angry, but he had missed seven autumns, and it wasn't the anger that brought him to the door with his shears in a bag the following Monday.

"You are old," the gardener said.

"Old and poor," said the rich man, "and worth only the price of weather."

The garden grew back in a shape that remembered having a shape. It is told that the rich man is no longer rich, but the gardener is no longer lonely.
