The Moon is far away. How far? About three hundred and eighty-four thousand kilometers, which means that all the planets of the solar system, lined up side by side, would fit between the Earth and the Moon with room to spare. It moves away from us by about four centimeters every year, stolen a little at a time from the slowing spin of the Earth, and a billion years from now the days will be longer and the total solar eclipses will be over forever. We live, unremarkably, in the brief era when the Moon looks exactly the size of the Sun in the sky, and nobody on any committee decided that.

A day on the Moon lasts about a month: two weeks of sunlight followed by two weeks of night. There are almost no sounds there because there is almost no air to carry them, and the footprints left by the twelve people who walked there will remain crisp for longer than most human buildings.

Sound is fast, but fussy about its road. In air at sea level it travels about three hundred and forty-three meters per second: close enough to one kilometer every three seconds, which is why counting between the lightning and the thunder works. In water it goes more than four times faster, because water molecules crowd together and pass the push along quickly, and in steel it travels fastest of all among the everyday materials — nearly six thousand meters per second, which is why you can hear a distant train through the rails long before you hear it through the air.

But in space, where there is almost nothing to push, sound stops entirely. The Sun is a continuous colossal noise, by any mechanical measure — and the space between here and there carries none of it. Astronauts on the Moon spoke to each other by radio even standing helmet-to-helmet, sandwiched between two inches of cosmos which will not conduct a greeting a single centimeter.
===
Ice is uncommon stuff, and this matters more than any other fact about your kitchen. Almost every substance on Earth shrinks as it solidifies; water, uniquely among the common substances, expands. That is why pipes burst in winter, why icebergs float, and why lakes freeze from the top down instead of the bottom up.

If ice sank, winter lakes would freeze solid from the bottom and every fish in the north would die each year; instead, the ice forms a floating lid, and the water underneath stays at four degrees, which is the temperature at which water is most dense. Penguins and trout alike are alive on the strength of what is, physically speaking, a rounding error in the shape of the water molecule. Life as a whole is built on the accident that frozen water insists on taking up more room than liquid water; there is no reason it had to be this way.

The Sahara is the largest hot desert on Earth, roughly the size of the United States, and it is growing and shrinking at the same time depending on which edge you look at. Despite the postcards, only about a fifth of it is sand dunes; most of it is gravel plain and bare rock. It is a desert not because it is hot but because the air over it is sinking: the great circulation cells of the atmosphere drop their moisture over the equator and descend, dried out, over the latitudes around thirty degrees, which is why the world's hot deserts come in two tidy bands north and south of the equator, like burn marks left and right of the middle.

Yet six thousand years ago the Sahara was green, with hippos in its lakes and people painting cattle on its rocks. The tilt of the Earth wobbles over tens of thousands of years, and the desert follows the wobble: it will be green again, on a schedule no civilization gets to watch.

Mammals and reptiles took different jobs at the end of the age of dinosaurs, and the difference between them comes down to how they pay for their heat. Reptiles are economical: they warm up in the sun and get on with business, which is cheap but means a cold reptile is a slow reptile. Mammals burn food to make their own heat around the clock, which is ruinously expensive — a mouse eats several times its weight every week — but makes them free of the weather, able to hunt mice in the snow at midnight.

That is the whole trade: reptiles rent their energy from the sun at a bargain, mammals own their furnaces outright at a staggering price. The feathered ones, borrowing here a trick from a lost dinosaur past, chose the mammal way too, and flew off on it.
===
The speed of light is the only speed limit the universe has been caught enforcing, at just under three hundred thousand kilometers per second. Nothing with mass can reach it, and everything without mass is required to travel at exactly it. Light from the Sun takes about eight minutes to arrive; from the nearest star system, four years; from the Andromeda galaxy, two and a half million years. When you look at Andromeda tonight from a dark yard, you see light that left when early humans were learning to throw stones.

This speed limit bends the universe to strange effects. The faster you travel, the slower your clock runs compared to your twin left at home — not metaphorically slower, actually slower, by amounts that GPS satellites have to be corrected for every day to keep giving you the right street corner. Physics students are surprised by this once; engineers correct for it for a living.

A teaspoon of healthy soil holds more living things than there are people on Earth: a working city of bacteria, fungi, mites, tiny worms, and insect larvae, all busy at the great recycling scheme of turning dead leaves back into live ones. Plants cannot eat soil; they eat what the soil's inhabitants release, atom by atom, from the remains of what died. The arrangement is so old that the green world above ground is really only the visible tip of the brown world's metabolism.

This is why the dust bowl of the 1930s was a catastrophe measured in centimeters: when the prairies were plowed to dust and the soil blew away, what blew away was not dirt but a food chain, and it does not reassemble on a farmer's schedule. Good land is made at the rate of perhaps a centimeter a century, spent at the rate of a bad decade.
===
Electricity arrived in the home slowly and then all at once. The first houses wired for power used it for exactly one purpose, light; a "socket" for anything else did not exist, and early vacuum cleaners came with a screw-in fitting to borrow the light bulb's supply. In one long lifetime the arrangement inverted: lighting became a minor line item, and heating, cooling, cooking, computing, and charging swallowed the rest, up to the point where a phone charger is now a more abstract and universal home object than a candle.

The grid that serves it all is, at any moment, a balanced equation: power generated equals power used, with no warehouse in between. Coal, gas, water, wind, sun, and the spinning of uranium-heated kettles all run a little ahead of demand, and somebody's job, in a room you will never see, is to watch that equation every second and keep the balance true.
===
Tides are the ocean being pulled in two directions at once by the Moon's uneven affection: the side of Earth facing the Moon is pulled hardest and bulges toward it; the far side is pulled least and bulges away from it, left behind as it were. Between the two bulges, coastlines experience roughly two high tides and two low tides a day.

The Sun does the same thing at half the strength; when Sun and Moon line up at new moon and full moon, their pulls combine into the big "spring" tides; at the quarter moons they cancel into weak little "neaps." Fishermen, sailors, and the architects of seaside power stations have respected this schedule for centuries without any need to know that its geometry was worked out by Newton, mostly as a side effect of a larger argument about apples.
===
Evolution has no plan and no direction, but it is not random the way rain on a pavement is random. Variation enters blindly, by mutation; survival and reproduction filter it with ruthless consistency, like a sieve; and what accumulates over staggering periods is the appearance of design by a designer who was never there. The eye, the wing, the flower, the enzyme: all were assembled out of whatever happened to be lying around, in the same way a city is assembled out of previous cities, brick by borrowed brick.

Natural selection explains, among other things, why bacteria become resistant to antibiotics, why island animals come in strange sizes, why the cuckoo's egg mimics the host's egg, why there are no rabbits the size of elephants, and why the giraffe's neck nerve makes a four-meter detour to travel thirty centimeters. None of these required foresight. They required only time, and time, in biology, is the one resource that never runs short.
