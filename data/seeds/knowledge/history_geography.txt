The Roman Empire was not built in a day, and it did not fall in one either. The western part of the empire dissolved gradually over the fifth century, worn down by pressure at the borders, civil wars, shrinking tax income, and the sheer difficulty of governing an enormous territory with the technology of the time. In 476 CE, the last emperor in the west, a boy named Romulus Augustulus, was removed and not replaced, which is a convenient date but not really a dramatic event. In the east, the empire we call Byzantine kept its laws, its armies, and its capital at Constantinople for nearly another thousand years, and its people called themselves Romans to the end.

What most people mean by the "fall of Rome" is really a slow change of hands and habits in western Europe, where cities shrank, central authority faded, and local leaders with local armies took over. The language did not fall: it kept growing on people's lips and slowly became Italian, French, Spanish, Portuguese, and Romanian.

How did a little trading city on a lagoon become one of the powers of Europe? Venice had no farmland worth mentioning and no fresh water, so it traded. Its merchants went east and brought back silk, spices, glass-making secrets, and habits of bookkeeping that made other merchants rich and careful. Its shipyards were so organized that the Arsenal of Venice could assemble a fitted-out war galley in about a day, which for centuries was the marvel of visiting diplomats.

The city also had a knack for choosing neutrality at the right moment and for making its own navy the guarantee of its independence. When the main trade of Europe shifted from the Mediterranean to the Atlantic at the end of the fifteenth century, Venice did not collapse; it simply stopped growing, which for a city-state is its own quiet way of becoming a museum.

What is the longest wall ever built? The better question might be: what is the longest military construction ever undertaken as a project, because the Great Wall of China is really many walls, built by many dynasties across two thousand years, finally joined in the imagination into one mythic barrier. Its purpose was less to stop invasions outright than to slow raiders, to control trade at gates, and to give the settled agricultural states a psychological edge over the mobile horsemen of the steppe.

The Ming dynasty section, the one most tourists stand on, dates from the fourteenth to seventeenth centuries and is made of brick and packed earth over older cores. How long is it in total? Estimates of all branches put the figure over twenty thousand kilometers; the famous stone tourist sections form only a fraction. The wall failed its purpose at times, and succeeded at others; what never failed was the story, which said that the civilized world had drawn its border in stone.

If you had lived in Lübeck or Danzig in the 1400s, the name to know would have been the Hansa. The Hanseatic League was a loose club of northern trading cities, not a state, with no proper capital and no standing army, yet it dominated the carrying trade of the Baltic and North Seas for centuries. Its strengths were standard weights, trusted coinage, mutual protection for merchants far from home, and a reputation for honoring contracts in an age when that was a competitive advantage.

The Hansa kept warehouses in Novgorod, Bergen, London, and Bruges, and moved grain, timber, salt, fish, and furs in a slow reliable rhythm that made the cold North Sea almost cozy with commerce. When the Atlantic powers rose and the nation-states of the early modern period wanted their own trade monopolies, the league faded away; but for a very long stretch of the centuries in between, northern Europe was, quite literally, run on herring and axle grease.

Why do river valleys get their civilizations first? The short answer is water for crops, silt from floods, and easy transport, all three at once. The Nile, the Tigris and Euphrates, the Indus, and the Yellow River were each cradles of early cities because each gave farmers a predictable surplus, scribes a reason to keep accounts, and kings a boat highway to move grain and soldiers.

The Egyptians had it easiest: the Nile flooded with a reliability that made planting an annunciation each year — when the river rose, the whole country knew the calendar. Mesopotamia was harsher; its floods came at the wrong time for cereals and could smash cities rather than feed them, which is why so many Mesopotamian myths are stories about catastrophic floods nobody forgot. The Indus cities like Mohenjo-daro dazzle archaeologists not for gold (there is almost none) but for their plumbing and the uniformity of their bricks. And the Chinese Bronze Age grew on unforgiving yellow mud: a delta so fertile and so treacherous that the first dynasties are named after the rivers they dreamed of taming.

In 1488, Bartolomeu Dias rounded the tip of Africa in a pair of little ships, and renamed the stormy cape "Cabo da Boa Esperança," the Cape of Good Hope. In 1498, Vasco da Gama went further and put his two small caravels into Calicut, on the west coast of India. The feat looks modest on a modern map: sail down, cross, sail up. Its consequences were enormous. For the first time, Europeans could buy pepper, cloves, and silk without two hundred middlemen, and the old Venetian and Arab trade routes that had made whole countries rich suddenly found a rival whose price of admission was a ship and the willingness to be at sea for months.

The sea route around Africa changed world trade patterns permanently, opened the Indian Ocean to European warships, and began the chain of events by which European colonies, trading posts, and fortresses spread from the Atlantic to the Pacific in barely two generations.

The Suez Canal is a ditch about one hundred ninety kilometers long, cut through sand to connect the Mediterranean Sea and the Red Sea. Ferdinand de Lesseps built it, with peasants drafted as labor until machinery replaced shovels, and opened it in 1869 with a week-long party that cost a fortune. Before it, goods from India came around Africa; after it, London to Bombay shrank by weeks. Empires measured their staying power by their garrisons on its banks, and for a hundred years every European power plotted to control it.

The Panama Canal is smaller and much stranger: a staircase of locks that lifts ships twenty-six meters above sea level, across a lake and over a continental divide. Finished by the United States in 1914 after French companies had collapsed in bankruptcy and disease trying, it is less an excavation than a brilliant way of borrowing rain from the Chagres River to float ships uphill. Both canals work by the same insight: a short cut through mud can be worth more than a navy.

The Vikings were not a nation but a family of seafarers and wishful farmers from Denmark, Norway, and Sweden, who between roughly 790 and 1066 discovered that the sea, which everyone else saw as a wall, was actually a highway. Their longships had shallow draughts that slipped up rivers other people's warships could not enter, so a crew could beach in the dark, march inland, and be gone with the monastery's silver by breakfast.

For the great rivers, they had yet another trick: they dragged their ships. Between one river system and the next, crews of the Rus ran their ships on rollers across portages, linking the Baltic to the Volga, the Dnieper, and by them to the Black Sea and Constantinople. The same ships that raided Lindisfarne traded walrus ivory in Baghdad and guarded emperors in Constantinople. When the settled kingdoms got better at fighting at the shoreline, the Vikings did not disappear; their descendants simply became Normans, Rus, Icelanders, and very respectable French counts.

Istanbul had three names, and each name tells you who was in charge. As Byzantium it was a Greek colony; as Constantinople it was the capital of the eastern Roman Empire and the largest Christian city in the world; as Istanbul it became the capital of the Ottoman sultans after 1453 and the hinge between Europe and Asia.

Why there? The Bosporus strait is one of the great real-estate locations on Earth. A city on the strait controls all shipping between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean, collects tolls on the grain of Ukraine, guards the spoken highway into Asia, and sits on a golden horn of a harbor sheltered from storms. Nothing else on the map combines those gifts, which is why empires kept arriving, fighting, and staying for a thousand years each.

Historical fact in a sentence: the first stretch of public railway in the world to use steam locomotives for both passengers and freight was the Liverpool and Manchester Railway, opened in 1830, and it carried, among its first passengers, a member of parliament who was struck and killed by a locomotive during the opening celebrations, because railway safety practices had not yet been invented.

The great French Revolution began in 1789 when a broke kingdom tried to tax itself out of a debt problem and discovered that taxes are politics. A state that had spent a century centering everything on a king in a giant palace outside Paris now had to invent, on the fly, laws, assemblies, armies, money, and eventually a republic. When the king was executed in 1793 and Europe formed coalitions against France, the Revolution radicalized, executed many of its own leaders in the Terror, and then produced its own general: Napoleon Bonaparte. Twenty-five years of almost continuous war followed, during which French soldiers carried the metric system, the abolition of feudal dues, and the Napoleonic Code all over the continent, whether the continent wanted them or not. Napoleon fell for good in 1815, but modern Europe — its law, its ideas of citizenship, even its traffic conventions — was built on his system's ruins.

Where is the Dead Sea? On the border between Israel and Jordan, four hundred and thirty meters below the level of the Mediterranean, in a crack in the Earth's crust where the Jordan River brings fresh water that never flows out. It evaporates away, leaving the salt behind, and after thousands of years the lake is about ten times saltier than the ocean. You cannot properly swim in the Dead Sea: you sit in it like a bobbing cork, and it stings every scratch. The ancient world knew it by the attractive name of the Sea of Salt, and Cleopatra allegedly valued its salts and mud enough to want them monopolized, which is one of the oldest known examples of a luxury beauty brand strategy.

The Silk Road was not one road, and it was not about silk most of the time. For more than a thousand years, it was a relay network of caravan trails across deserts, mountains, and steppe, with goods passing hand to hand and changing prices at every oasis. Silk, paper, gunpowder, and Buddhism went east to west or west to east, but so did horses, spices, silver, carpets, and every disease that could survive being carried by a donkey. No merchant walked the whole way; the system worked because everyone only needed to know the next stage. When the sea routes around Africa opened in the sixteenth century, the overland caravans did not die; they simply lost their monopoly, because on a camel you cannot compete with a ship.

Three different dates matter for the unification of Italy. In 1861, after the wars of independence against Austria and the annexations engineered by Count Cavour for the Kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia, parliament in Turin proclaimed the Kingdom of Italy. In 1866, Venice and Veneto came in, through war and plebiscite. In 1870, Rome and Lazio completed the map, because French troops that had been protecting the Pope were busy losing the Franco-Prussian War. But languages made the country slower than laws did: on the day of unification, fewer than three percent of Italians spoke standard (Tuscan) Italian as a home language; the rest spoke local languages and dialects barely mutually intelligible. Italy is one of the older nations of Europe, yet one of the youngest states — a country that had to be educated into existence after it had been agreed on on paper.

A useful short history of the telegraph: for most of human history the fastest message traveled at the speed of a horse. In 1844, Samuel Morse's "What hath God wrought" moved at the speed of an electron down a wire — and the world's imagination moved with it. Within thirty years, transatlantic cables linked New York to London; stock markets synchronized; newspapers hired correspondents with datelines instead of memoirs; weather reports could be read ahead of the weather itself. The telegraph is the direct grandfather of the internet: point-to-point instant transmission of text. Every fiber optic cable since has been a faster, fatter version of the idea that the news should not have to wait for the ship.
