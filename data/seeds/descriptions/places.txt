The harbor at dawn looked as if someone had drawn it in pencil and forgotten to ink it. The water was the color of pewter, flat and fine, and the hulls of the fishing boats sat on it without a ripple between them. Masts made thin lines against a sky that had not decided yet whether to be gold or gray. Out by the wall, a single lamp still burned, and its reflection reached toward the shore like a long orange finger, pointing at the day.

In the middle of the old town there was a square that had clearly grown rather than been planned. No two of its four sides agreed about anything: one was tall houses with iron balconies, one was a church with a round door like a surprised mouth, one was a row of chestnut trees holding up a green ceiling, and the fourth was a fountain older than all of them together. At noon the square filled with shopping baskets and arguments about produce; at dusk it emptied into the sound of water and the lamplighter's cart. Every town has a place where it keeps its heart, and for that town it was the square with the crooked fountain.
===
The mountain road climbed in long loops, each one a little quieter and a little colder than the last. Below, the valley was green and busy, fields like a quilt someone had patched from every shade of green there is. Halfway up, the houses thinned to one farmhouse with smoke climbing straight as a ruler from its chimney. Then even the trees gave out — first the tall ones, then the twisted little ones clinging to the rocks — and the road was left alone with the wind, the sheep that pretended not to hear it, and finally, above everything, the white silence.
===
The reading room smelled of old paper and lemon wax, a smell that made people lower their voices without being told. Light came down through tall arched windows in pale columns, each column full of slow-drifting dust. The shelves went up two stories, with a wooden gallery running around the second, and the brass ladder on its rail stood waiting by the atlases. In the far corner, under the green glass lamps, four students and one pensioner sat around the big oak table, and the turning of their pages was the only conversation the room required.
===
The river made the town's decisions for it. It decided where the mill stood, and where the bridge, and how far apart the two rows of houses could afford to face each other. In summer it ran slow and green around the piles of the bridge, with boys fishing off the downstream side and laundry drying on lines above it. In autumn it rose and darkened and carried whole small trees through the town at a stately pace, like furniture being moved. Everyone in the town looked at it first thing in the morning, the way you check on a family member with a temper.
===
The farmhouse kitchen was the largest room in the house and the only one that mattered. A black iron range took up the whole of one wall, and above it hung strings of onions and a row of pans polished by decades of hands. The table in the middle was so old and scrubbed that the grain of the wood stood up like the ridges of a field. By the window, herbs grew in chipped enameled mugs, and under the window sat the dog, who had learned that the floor there was always warm. Whatever happened elsewhere in the house — births and deaths and winters — the kitchen simply kept being warm and full of bread, like a small country with sensible laws.
===
Beyond the last street of the village was a path that did not seem to lead anywhere, which is precisely why it led everywhere worth going. It began between two hawthorns, crossed a stile polished by fifty years of trousers, and then disappeared pleasantly into a meadow where it was nobody's business to keep track of it. In June the path was a tunnel of green shade with the smell of wild garlic. In November it was a line of puddles stitched across a gray field. It was the same path, of course; it was just in disguise, like the village, like the year, like everyone who walked it pretending to be having an ordinary day.
===
The old train station had two platforms and one great clock that had been eight minutes fast since before anyone could remember, which the town regarded not as a fault but as a personality. The roof over the platforms was made of iron ribs and smoky glass, and when a train came in the whole structure boomed like a drum. On the southbound platform there was a willow basket of a kiosk selling newspapers and hard candy, and beside it a chest-high stove that glowed all winter, around which the village's old men gathered to discuss the trains, the weather, and the government, in that order of importance.
===
The cellar under Mrs. Okafor's shop was older than the shop and maybe older than the street. The walls were thick stone, sweating faintly in all seasons, and the floor was brick laid by hands that had been dust for two hundred years. Racks of preserves and pickles ran along one wall in careful rows labeled in Mrs. Okafor's severe handwriting. At the far end, where the light never reached, there was a small bricked-up arch whose purpose nobody could agree on. The cat, who was allowed to hunt down there among the decades, refused to cross the line where the light ended, and Mrs. Okafor, who believed in the cat more than in most historians, had never once asked it to.
===
Dawn on the farm began before the light. First came the sounds: a chain on the barn door, a bucket, a cow registering a mild complaint. Then the eastern sky went from charcoal to a pale wash of rose behind the elms, and the fields appeared in sections, like a curtain pulled aside by degrees. Mist stood in the low pasture, flat and white as a lake, with the backs of two sheep rising out of it like small gray islands. By the time the sun cleared the hill, the farm had already been awake for an hour and was beginning, in its quiet way, to be smug about it.
===
The bazaar occupied four crooked lanes in the oldest part of the city, and it was less a place than a weather system. Heat gathered under the low awnings, and so did sound: the hammering of the coppersmiths in the first lane, the calls of the spice sellers in the second, the hum of bargaining everywhere like a river that never quite surfaces. Colors worked harder there than anywhere else in town — pyramids of oranges beside heaps of red peppers beside stacks of blue-dyed cloth — and the light fell in bright blades between the awnings, dividing the crowd into stripes. To walk through without buying anything took twenty minutes and a will of iron.
===
The island was small enough to walk around in an hour, and it held exactly one of everything: one village, one shop, one church, one school, one harbor with one boat that went to the mainland every day except the days it didn't. The wind, however, was plural, abundant, and spent the whole year testing the island's roof tiles and the islanders' patience. In summer the whole place smelled of salt and sun-warmed thrift, a flower that grew out of the cliff tops in impossible pink drifts. In winter the mail boat came when it could, and the village gathered at the harbor to greet it like a relative who was always welcome and never reliable.
===
The workshop was long and narrow, with a bench down one side and a wall of tools down the other, each tool hanging in its own painted outline so that a missing one announced itself like an absent tooth. Sawdust lay thin over everything, softening the edges of the years. Above the bench a small window looked across the yard to the apple tree, and the joiner had so arranged it that in the hour before the light failed, when the work was at its most demanding, a square of late sun fell exactly on the vice. It is a mistake to suppose craftsmen do not think about such things; they think about nothing else when you are not there.
===
At the head of the valley stood the dam, a great gray shoulder of concrete holding back a mile of still water. From the road above, the lake looked calm enough to walk on, and the mountains leaned over it in a ring, softened by distance into blue and then into rumor. But stand on the dam wall itself and you felt the weight of the thing — a depth of dark water behind you moving in its sleep, and below, through the curved and shining spillway, the river being born all over again in a curtain of white noise. The attendant at the little gatehouse said that in flood season the whole valley hummed, and that he had long ago stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing your own heart.
===
There was a garden behind the house on Byron Street that no one admitted to tending. It had a plum tree that had stopped bothering to bear plums, a rose that made up for the plum with unreasonable heroism, and a narrow bed where somebody's bulbs came up each spring in strict rows regardless of who had planted them. The neighbors said the old owner had planted everything and that the garden simply continued out of loyalty. The birds, who knew the garden's secrets better than anyone, said nothing, which is what birds always say when they are winning.
