The violin maker was a small man with enormous hands, which puzzled everyone until they saw him work; the hands, it turned out, were simply large enough to be gentle. He moved around his workshop as if the floor were covered in eggshells, and when he spoke he sounded the way his varnish looked — careful, even, and slow-drying. His hair had retreated years ago and left a smooth dome that caught the lamplight, and his apron was so old it had molded to the shape of him. He hummed when he worked, always the same six notes, and stopped only when a joint was giving him trouble, as if the wood had noticed the song falter and grown sullen too.

Grandmother Iveta kept her whole history in her face if you knew how to read it, and she would teach you, if you were patient. A deep line between her brows was where the hard winters had gone. Lines around her eyes were from laughing, and also from squinting at threads, planting, letters, and grandchildren long enough to laugh at all of them. Her hands were wide, the knuckles now generous, and every finger had a story it could tell about a knife, a needle, or a bramble. She wore her hair in a braid wrapped around her head the way her mother had, and the braid had gone from black to iron to snow, and she was proud of the snow in a way that made young women envious.

The boy was thirteen that summer, and everything about him was in the middle of changing except his patience, which was already the longest thing in the household. He had grown so fast his wrists were always a month ahead of his sleeves. His voice traveled between the old one and the new one like a ferry that had not yet fixed its schedule. He listened more than he talked, and when he did talk, adults found themselves answering him as if he were one of them, which flustered him pleasantly. That summer he discovered two things about himself: that he could swim the river, and that he could watch the sun go down behind the hills without once wishing to be entertained better.

The brass compass had belonged to her grandfather, and it was beautiful the way certain tools are beautiful when they have spent a century being exactly what they are. The case was worn smooth at the hinge where a thumb had rubbed it while leaning over maps and across time zones. Inside, the dial floated on a bead of liquid the color of weak tea, and the needle swung up to north the way a well-trained dog comes when called. There was a dent on the rim from Constantinople, or possibly from a train platform in Vienna; the compass knew and was not telling. What it told, instead, was always the same: north this way, everything else relative to that, and don't worry.

The auctioneer was the calmest man in the room by profession, and he made a theater of that calm. He stood behind the lectern in a suit cut a little too loose, so that he had somewhere to keep his arms, and he read the crowd the way other men read racing pages. A twitch of the catalogue, a pause in the repetition of the price, a tiny lift of one eyebrow toward the telephone bidder's assistant — the whole ballet existed to make a hundred people in a stuffy room feel they were each one breath away from the object of their dreams. When the hammer came down, he smiled as if the results had surprised even him.

The photograph album was a heavy cloth volume with brass corners, the kind built to survive the people it described. Its pages were black and thick as card, and the pictures were held by little paper corners that Sera's mother had glued in with the concentration of a surgeon. The very first page was a wedding picture from before the turn of the century, the bride unsmiling in the formal way of that time, the groom holding his hat as if the camera might steal it. Below it, in small white letters faded to gray, someone had written: 'They were happy. I know this because she told me.' The album was full of that handwriting, insisting gently that everything be remembered correctly.

Mr. Penshaw, who kept the lighthouse and the modest flock of sheep on the point, had learned to talk so little that his few words were always interesting. He wore the same oilskin coat from October to April, not because he had no other but because, he once explained to a visiting journalist, 'this one knows the wind.' His beard was the color of brine soaked rope, and his eyes, pale as the horizon, spent most of their time somewhere beyond whatever you were showing them. He communicated with the supply boat through flags, with his dog through whistles that meant six different things, and with his sister on the mainland through letters of exactly one page, written on Sundays and mailed on Mondays without exception.

The old umbrella was a ridiculous object and everyone who saw it understood that Aunt Heloise would never part with it. Once it had been elegant, black silk stretched over ribs so fine they might have been borrowed from a bird; now the silk was faded to the color of weak coffee, two ribs sprouted in unexpected directions, and one had been repaired with wire that had no business being trusted. The handle was of amber-colored resin, smooth at the grip from a million rainy walks. She carried it in sun as often as in rain, pointed at things with it, used it to encourage slow pedestrians, and once used it to defend a duck from a terrier, an event the street remembered for a decade.

The nurse moved through the night ward in soft shoes, and the patients knew her by the sound of her keys before they saw her. Her hair was pinned so firmly at the start of every shift that even the emergencies of dawn could not shake it loose; it was said that she had been seen to repin it while discussing dosages with one hand. She had a specific smile for the patients who complained the most, the one that acknowledged the complaint and filed it away for later, when, as she put it, 'we will have a proper argument that we both win.' The ward was a difficult place, full of small griefs, and she treated it like a small country that deserved a good constitution and daily elections.

The box was made of cedar, a deep reddish wood that smelled of forests older than the house it sat in. The lid fitted with the patience of wood that had been planed by someone who had all week, and it closed with a sound like a very quiet certainty. Inside, it held the ordinary treasures of one family for three generations: a baby tooth in a twist of paper, a medal from a war that had ended before the current owners were born, six foreign coins that nobody could place, a key that opened something no one had identified, two wedding rings that had outlasted the marriages they belonged to, and a letter in an envelope marked 'to be opened on your eighteenth birthday' addressed to a daughter the family had never had.

The choir conductor was a spare, straight-backed woman who never raised her voice — she raised her hands, and the sound obeyed by force of pure expectation. Her hearing was legendary: she could stop a sixty-voice choir with a frown and identify the precise errant alto, name, section, and note. The basses adored her with the deep seriousness of basses everywhere; the tenors feared and craved her approval in roughly equal warmth. At concerts, she stood for a breath with her hands at her sides before beginning, and every singer in the room leaned toward that silence the way plants lean toward the sun, and then the music began, exactly as if the silence had been conducting all along.

The cottage door was a joyful contradiction to the rest of the village. It was painted a blue so bright that on foggy days it looked like a gap in the weather. The hinges were oiled to silence, the brass knocker — a fox's head — was polished weekly, and the stone step in front had been scrubbed for so many decades that it bowed gently in the middle like a spine bent in welcome. The owner repainted the blue every other year from a can kept under the stairs, and repainting day was a small local holiday during which she allowed children to watch, bribed them with ginger biscuits, and never, ever allowed them to touch the brush.

The deaf shepherd had worked the same hills for forty years, and his dogs worked for him the way interpreters work for presidents – shuttling, precise, and full of professional pride. He spoke to them in signs and in soft pops of his lips against his teeth, and they read him from astonishing distances, one ear cocked toward his whistle even though he could not blow one. The village understood him in the ways that mattered: a nod for sheep sold, a flat hand for rain coming if the wind kept south, two fingers to the brim of his hat for thank you. The youngest schoolteacher, who had learned signing from her grandmother, said his sentences in sign were the most economical poetry she had ever seen anyone perform.

The old typewriter on the desk dated from before the war, and he used it from pride and from stubbornness, and because, he always said, 'it makes me write slower than I regret.' It was a heavy machine with crescent keys that fought back a little, forcing every writer to mean it. The ribbon went from black to red in the middle of 'fecund,' sometimes inconveniently. Around it his desk was a paper archipelago — islands of letters, envoys from the mainland of the typewriter. When the keys stuck, which they did when humidity or carelessness accumulated, he would stop, clean them lovingly with a pin, and remark to nobody that had the typewriter been a general, the war would have gone differently.

She painted the sea from memory, having lived beside it for sixty-one years, and her paintings looked more like the sea than the sea did on most days. Her studio was the back bedroom of a fisherman's cottage, and the light entered there as if invited — northern, steady, the color of cool water. Her hands had begun to tremble the year before, so she had simply changed her style: the brushes got longer, the strokes got bolder, and the stormy days she painted got better. Young painters who visited asked for her secret. She tapped her temple with the end of the brush: 'I've got sixty years of sea in here. You come back when you've got twenty, and we'll talk about light.'
