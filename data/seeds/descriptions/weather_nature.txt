The first real frost of winter arrived in the night and got everywhere. It silvered the fallen leaves until they looked like castings in a foundry, crisp around the edges and still damp-hearted underneath. The garden gate had grown a white pelt, and the last apples on the tree wore small pale jackets of ice. When the sun came up the frost did not melt so much as surrender slowly, starting with the gate latch, where a thousand hands had made the metal warm by tradition. By ten the lawn was rustling again instead of crunching, and only the shadows kept the cold.
===
The thunderstorm came over the hills in the late afternoon with a kind of ceremony. First the light went wrong, becoming brassy and theatrical, so that the white farmhouses glowed against the darkening west as if lit from inside. The birds had decided about the storm before the people had, and the sky, full of swifts an hour before, was suddenly empty of everything but cloud. Then the thunder began, still far away, a deep rolling like furniture being rearranged upstairs; then the wind arrived in the garden all at once, bending the hollyhocks; and then the rain hit the windows in handfuls, as if thrown. The whole performance lasted forty minutes, and afterward the world smelled of dust that had finally been given water.
===
Spring came to the river valley not as a date on a calendar but as a series of small recoveries, each one easy to miss. The elms budded first, a faint smoky green seen at a distance that was not yet leaves. Then the water ran clearer, because the dirt of the winter was finally bound by new roots. Then the frogs found the marsh pond, all on the same two nights, so that the sound of them rose like a kettle coming to boil in stages. Last came the swallows, back to the same barn eaves from which they had inspected the previous summer; the farm dogs noticed them before the people did, heads up, all four of them, watching for the streaks of blue-black against the morning.
===
The heat of midsummer lay over the streets of the town like a great soft animal, heavy and fond of the shade. In the afternoon, everything slowed to the temperature: the traffic, the cats, the conversation on the benches under the lindens. The air above the pavement wavered, and the church bells sounded as if they had been rung underwater. People moved from shade to shade the way you move between stepping-stones across a river. Only the fountain played happily in the square, white against the blue, and the children, who were fuelled by a different sun entirely, ran in and out of its spray for hours.
===
Autumn arrived carrying its two arguments in the same basket. The first argument said that the world was ending, and brought evidence: leaves letting go by the thousands, flowers folding their shops, the sun quitting work earlier every day. The second argument said, not ending, storing; and it brought its own evidence: the smell of apples stacked in cellars, the fattening of squirrels, the slow perfect ripening of the light itself, which turned from yellow to amber and made everything it touched look briefly like the thing most worth keeping. The two arguments, as everyone knows who has lived through more than six autumns, have always both won.
===
Fog came down on the harbor in the morning so thick that the town's geography had to be done by ear. The water was there by the sound of gulls. The pier was there by the sound of boots on boards. The white tower of the lighthouse, which stood tall enough to be seen twenty miles on a normal day, was entirely absent, and in its place its horn called out over the water with a long, patient, mournful note, because the tower had lost its eyes and been given a voice instead. Ships hung somewhere invisible and spoke to each other in careful bursts of horn. By eleven the sun was working on the fog from above, steaming it away strand by strand, and the town slowly reassembled itself, shining like a photograph being developed.
===
In the pine forest after rain, the silence was softer than in any room ever built. The ground swallowed footsteps whole, being upholstered with needles in the years-long work of carpet-making. Shafts of light came through the trees at long angles, each one populated by slow-drifting specks, as if the sun had run a curtain rod between two trunks and poured half a honey-jar down it. Every breath tasted of resin and clean moss. Far off somewhere a woodpecker knocked in patient Morse, and a jay, the rude relative of forest birds, objected to something loudly and briefly and was ignored by everyone.
===
The first snow came quietly, overnight, and the morning discovered the whole town changed into a different instrument. Roofs were white, fences were white-ridged, the red pillar-box at the corner had acquired a tall white hat. Sounds came back muffled and more distant, as if the snow had put the town under a great glass dome for safekeeping. Breath made small clouds; boots made creaks. The first children outside made the first prints, purposefully where the snow was deepest, from the instinct older than any city to write your name on a page while it's still clean. By noon the road was black again and ordinary, but along the rooftops the snow stayed, memorizing the sun.
===
The wind that afternoon had a destination and was in a hurry to get there. It drove down the main street in powerful, invisible hands-on-shoulders gusts, taking hats, rearranging the market's display cloths, delivering leaves from the west side of town to the east side free of charge. The weather vane on the chapel, which on normal days turned with the dignity of a magistrate, spun like something being berated. Trees along the river leaned and straightened, leaned and straightened, doing a slow dance they practiced every windfall but never quite perfected. In the sheltered lanes, where no wind reached, the fallen leaves kept their places exactly, as if the storm was a rumor happening elsewhere.
===
The coast at low tide offered a geography lesson written in rock pools and exposed ribs of worn stone. The sea had gone out so far that the harbor floor lay bare, patched in brown weed and glistening puddles. Gulls walked the mud with their feet printing small exclamations, looking for whatever the sea had forgotten to take. Each pool was a small world with its own weather: minute crab traffic, anemones blooming like underwater dahlias, a single silver baby fish circling its whole universe in a panic you could calm by simply letting the time pass. When the tide turned, the line of it was visible from far out, returning in a rippling blue-green wall that made the keeper of the lighthouse look at his watch and nod.
===
The dawn chorus began at first light with one bird, and grew in the way that rumors of a good thing grow. First the robin, whose territory was the garden by the wall, sang alone in the blue half-dark — four or five notes at a time, spaced with the patience of a bell. Then the blackbird in the pear tree, who fancied himself the better musician, took up his longer, more honeyed variations. Then the sparrows arrived at the business end of the morning, all questions and answers at once, and somewhere high above them the skylark simply climbed the air and sang from the top of the sky as though altitude were part of the melody. By six, the whole village roofscape was a concert hall filling for the main performance, which was the day.
===
Night in the desert was astronomer's weather: clear, cold, and immeasurably deep. The stars were not scattered across the sky but stacked in layers, so dense and so far back that looking at them felt like looking down from a great height rather than up. The Milky Way ran from one end of the darkness to the other like smoke that had forgotten to move. The desert, which at noon had shimmered with heat, radiated the last of its warmth upward past the traveler and into the black, and the traveler sat listening to the crack of cooling stones and the small, busy sounds of night creatures that had spent the whole day waiting for the sun to mind its own business.
===
After the first heavy rain of the season, the dry river bed south of the village transformed overnight from a geography into a rumor of a river. Dark water stood in the deepest pools. The gravel had turned from pale to iron and smelled — this was the strangest thing — washed, as if the whole bed had been waiting to be laundered. By morning the willow herbs along the banks, which had stood gray-green and patient through ten weeks of sun, looked so vividly refreshed that they seemed to have caught up on months of lost growing just by believing in it. Two herons had arrived to see what the change brought. It brought fish, though how the fish knew the river had returned, nobody could satisfactorily explain.
===
The moon rose full over the orchard and drew the trees twice — once in branches, once in shadows. Every blossom of them was lit from within like Chinese paper, and the whole orchard gave off a cool, faint radiance, as if it were the one providing the moonlight and not the moon. In the grass at the bottom of the slope, dew was forming with great thoroughness, so that each blade acquired its own small crystal lamp. A fox came over the ridge at the top, paused at the fence line with a paw raised, took in the light, the orchard, the order of things, and decided against whatever it had come to do. It turned and went back over the ridge, its brush a single bright stroke against the silver field, and the orchard kept the light without needing to say a word about it.
