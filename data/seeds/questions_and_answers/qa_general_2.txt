Q: What is the difference between poetry and prose?
A: Prose marches in sentences and paragraphs, and its uniform is ordinary speech; poetry wears rhythm, sound, and compressed line-lengths as deliberate instruments. Prose can afford to be scanned quickly; poetry asks to be seen and heard at the same time. But the border is porous: the best prose often sings, and the best poems usually tell something. One definition I trust: prose is built for clarity, while poetry is built for intensity — and both, when done well, respect the reader's time.

Q: Why do leaves turn red but some trees stay yellow?
A: The short answer is that trees come with different pigments in their wardrobes, and not all wardrobes contain red. All summer the leaf's workhorse green dominates, but yellow and orange pigments have been present the whole time, quietly assisting. When autumn comes and the green is withdrawn for storage, the yellows simply remain visible. Reds, however, must be manufactured fresh from the sugars trapped in the dying leaf — a costly investment that only some species make, possibly as protection or as plain chemistry. Maples budget for crimson; birches cash out quietly into gold.

Q: How do migrating geese navigate?
A: They use everything, all at once, one system backing up the other: the slant of the sun corrected by the clock in their blood, the pattern of stars learned when young, the important rivers and ridgelines memorized from earlier journeys, and—most astonishing to us—a sense of the Earth's magnetic field written into their eyes and beaks. A young goose makes its first migration in a convoy of experienced parents and is, in effect, a trainee pilot flying with instructors. When the instructors are gone, the young ones know the way.

Q: What is the difference between a violin and a fiddle?
A: None at all, physically. The difference is cultural: the same wooden box with four strings is a violin when played by a conservatoire graduate in a black suit and a fiddle when played at a barn dance by someone who learned from their grandmother. The players refer to different repertoires, endorse different tunings, and may even spend all evening insulting each other's style — but the pawnshop will charge you the same for either.

Q: Where do the biggest waves come from: storms close by or storms far away?
A: Mostly from storms far away, oddly enough. A distant storm at sea generates waves of all sizes, and as they travel, the smaller ones dissipate while the long, heavy swells keep rolling. Days later, at some peaceful coast with no cloud in the sky, enormous swells arrive from a storm a thousand miles away like tall visitors who traveled quietly and arrive looking for beds. Close storms make chop; distant ones, surf.

Q: When is 'fewer' correct instead of 'less'?
A: Use 'fewer' for things you can count one by one: fewer people, fewer bottles, fewer hours of sleep. Use 'less' for quantities you measure rather than count: less water, less noise, less time. The sign saying 'ten items or fewer' is expanding its literacy campaign from the checkout; the sign saying 'less items' is charming but imprecise. It matters because the meter-running kinds of English agree: when precision costs nothing, spend it.

Q: Can you explain why yawning is contagious?
A: Science has theories but not a verdict. Yawning seems linked to cooling the brain and to alertness, so the social waves of yawning might be an ancient, shared alertness signal among group animals: you yawn, I yawn, the tribe stirs. What is certain is that the signal does not require consciousness to spread — most of us yawn merely at the word 'yawn', and you, reader, have probably just proven it.

Q: What would happen if a mountain formed in your town?
A: Aside from the obvious remodeling of local real estate, the next thing to change would be the weather. Mountains force the air to climb, the climbing air cools, and the cooling air drops its moisture — so wet forests grow on the windward side while downwind a dry shadow forms, sometimes a desert. Rivers would reroute around it, roads would climb it in zigzags, wind would comb its ridgeline, and the town's identity would quietly bend to match its new silhouette, as towns always do.

Q: What is a hedgehog's defense worth, biologically speaking?
A: Considerable. A hedgehog is a small, slow, nocturnal insect-eater whose entire survival plan, upon meeting danger, is to become inedible. Its thousands of spines are modified hairs of stiffened keratin, and the muscles beneath can pull the whole spiky jacket tight in a fraction of a second, presenting nothing but seams. Against foxes, badgers, and most dogs it works wonderfully. Its main enemy in the modern world is the automobile, which is immune to keratin and to bravery alike.

Q: How does a battery 'die' of old age?
A: Inside a battery are chemical ingredients that were balanced eagerly against each other at the factory, wanting to react, with the device using that wanting to do work. Over the years, small side reactions steal a little of the eager material: layers grow at the borders that make it harder for the chemistry to proceed; tiny crystals bridge where they shouldn't; the patient, eager distance grows shorter. Eventually, though most of the original energy is chemically still on board, it can no longer flow as designed. The battery is not empty; it is merely retired.

Q: What is the difference between astronomy and astrology?
A: Astronomy is the patient measuring of the sky: positions, motions, temperatures, compositions, and the physics with which those facts are woven into models. Astrology is the much older practice of reading personal destiny into the positions of the planets, a practice universal among civilizations because it flatters our instinct for meaning. The two parted company when telescopes arrived, and they have not reconciled. Nothing in the sky has ever been shown to object.

Q: Why does thunder have different voices?
A: Thunder is a single arrival of sound stretched over distance, and the stretching is the source of its variety. A nearby crack of lightning is a single clap that reaches you practically all at once — sharp, startling, short. A distant bolt's sound arrives from a path many miles long, with parts of the sound taking slightly different routes through cold and warm air, near hills and far ones, and the brief crack arrives as a long, slow rumble. The rolling becomes richer for every obstacle it meets. A thunderstorm over a city can be heard talking to itself for twenty seconds about a single bolt.

Q: How do libraries decide which books to keep?
A: Better-run ones never guess: they count. Each book earns its shelf space through being borrowed, and a book that has not left the shelf in two or three years will be weighed against candidates for its spot. Classics earn a kind of tenure because their value is long-lasting, while the recent thriller that had its season may be culled to make room. The librarian's word for this is 'deaccessioning', and it is practiced with more sorrow than newspapers suppose. The truest library will always have a slightly invisible ongoing conversation between the books that stay and the books that go.

Q: What is the difference between a holly and an oak in a storm?
A: The holly's portrait is a hedge written by wind: dense, slow, and equipped with spined, glossy leaves that hold their shape and let the blow slip through the shrub like water. The oak is the opposite philosophy — a great stiff lever that meets the storm's full argument. Small storms it wins; the rare, great storm it can lose, catastrophically, by uprooting. The thorny shrub's flexibility is one kind of answer to the same wind that the pillar of the forest answers in another, and the hedgerow and the forest are both proof that nature publishes multiple solutions on the same page.

Q: How would you explain democracy to a child?
A: I would say: imagine a huge family too big to meet around one table, that still needs to agree on what to have for dinner and where to build the new playground. Democracy is the family's method: everyone old enough to understand the question gets a mark on a paper, the papers are counted honestly, and the biggest pile decides. It is not the fastest method, nor always the wisest, but it is the only method yet invented where losing one dinner vote doesn't mean you stop belonging to the family.

Q: Why do old books smell the way they do?
A: That famous perfume is slow chemistry politely announcing itself. The paper in older books is wood pulp, which contains compounds that break down over decades into an bouquet of smaller molecules — some sweet like vanilla, others sharp like almonds, others grassy. Heat, sunlight, and damp hurry the process along. The smell of an old book is literally the book aging in the air around you, a measurable signature of time passing through cellulose. Collectors pay for it; conservators try to stop it, to the chagrin of noses everywhere.

Q: What is the difference between a gulf and a bay?
A: Only grace and degree. Both are bites taken out of a coastline; a gulf is generally the bigger, deeper bite, often enclosed by a narrow mouth open to the wider ocean, while a bay is smaller, gentler, and opens broadly to the sea. The Gulf of Mexico gathers several nations into its arms; the bay at the end of our lane is a comma of sand sheltering a single rowboat. There is no formal border between the two words, and geography reserves its right to be local and inconsistent.

Q: Can you explain what insulation does in a house wall?
A: Insulation does not make the wall warm; it makes warmth slow. A wall made mostly of air-capturing material — wool, foam, cork, trapped fibers — is a wall through which heat must travel by a thousand tiny sluggish steps instead of one occasion. In winter the insulation holds the expensive indoor heat to its side of the boundary; in summer it holds the outdoor heat off from the cool interior. It is less a heater than a bouncer at a door, and it always works in both directions even though we only thank it when it's cold.

Q: What would happen if the world's clocks were all twelve hours wrong?
A: At first, harmless confusion: people would miss trains, arrive at work in the dark, and get hungry at the wrong tables. But nature reads no clocks. The sun would still rise, bees would still find flowers on their own solar schedule, tides would keep meeting the moon, and by the next generation humans would simply have adjusted which numbers they associate with morning. The deeper lesson is that clocks are a language, not a law — and languages, unlike tides, can be persuaded.

Q: Who wrote the fairy tales about the little mermaid and the ugly duckling?
A: Hans Christian Andersen, a Danish author of the nineteenth century, who traveled through Europe carrying a talent that fooled everyone at first: the son of a shoemaker and a washerwoman, he failed at acting and was laughed out of drawing rooms before his stories found the entire world. He invented many of his tales rather than collecting them from folk tradition, which is why they carry a particular voice — tender, a little melancholic, and strangely sharp about how people treat each other. He died famous, translated into every language that can house children.
