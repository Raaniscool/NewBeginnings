Q: What is the difference between a duck and a goose?
A: Both are water birds, but the goose is built on a larger frame with a longer neck, and it walks on land more elegantly than the waddling duck. Ducks quack in the manners we all know from picture books, while geese honk, and it is the goose, not the duck, that kept watch with its voice in ancient Rome. Their diets differ too: ducks sift the shallows for whatever lives there, while geese graze on grass like small feathered cattle.

Q: Why does bread go stale?
A: Bread stales by a slow rearranging of its starches rather than by mere drying. In fresh bread the starch is soft and disordered; over hours, the starch settles into tidy crystals and firms up from the inside, pushing moisture toward the crust as it goes. The process happens fastest at slightly cool temperatures, which is why the refrigerator is the worst place to keep bread, and why a freezer, which stops the crystals from settling, actually preserves freshness.

Q: How does a mirror work?
A: A mirror is glass with a thin skin of polished metal behind it, and its job is organized reflection. When light strikes an ordinary wall, the wall's rough texture scatters the rays every which way, so the wall sends you back a blur. When the same light strikes the mirror's smooth metal surface, each ray bounces off at the same tidy angle it arrived, and the pattern of the world is handed back intact. The mirror's surface does not contain a picture; it merely returns to your eye the picture the light was already carrying.

Q: Where is the wind fastest: at the shore or in a forest?
A: At the shore, by a wide margin. Open water offers the wind a smooth runway with nothing to catch on, so air arriving from the sea keeps its speed. A forest is the opposite: every trunk, branch, and leaf is a tiny brake, and the wind spends itself rubbing through a million green hands. This is why sailors love coasts and paragliders launch from ridges, while old forests shelter orchids that could not survive a stiff breeze.

Q: When did people first begin farming?
A: The first careful plantings happened roughly ten to twelve thousand years ago, and like most great inventions, farming began in more than one place independently. In the Middle East, people sowed wheat and barley on the hills above the great rivers. In China, early farmers domesticated rice along the wet lowlands. In New Guinea, in the Andes, and in the Americas, people settled down with their own local plants in their own good time. Nobody held a referendum; the change crept up on humanity one tended plot at a time.

Q: Who invented the wheelbarrow?
A: The wheelbarrow was developed in China, where it carried loads with a large central wheel for centuries before the design rolled westward. It is a humbler machine than the wheel itself but almost as clever: by placing the wheel under the load instead of behind it, the wheelbarrow lets the frail geometry of a single worker carry what would once have required two. It remains, to this day, the world's most refined method of making one back do the work of three.

Q: Can you explain what a rainbow is made of?
A: A rainbow is made of sunlight and raindrops and your exact position, and it belongs to you personally. Each raindrop acts as a tiny prism: sunlight enters the drop, bends, splits into its seven colors, reflects off the back of the drop, and bends again on its way out. Millions of drops do this together, but only the drops at a precise angle from your eyes send their split colors to you, so every observer sees a slightly different rainbow. The sky does not hold a rainbow; each pair of eyes assembles its own.

Q: What would happen if all volcanoes stopped erupting forever?
A: The short answer is that the planet would slowly lose one of its great recycling systems. Eruptions bring fresh rock, minerals, and gases from inside the Earth to the surface, and over geological time they help replenish the carbon in the air that keeps the world warm enough for liquid water. A volcano-free Earth would not freeze tomorrow, but over millions of years it might run very cold and very still. Occasional fire, it turns out, is one of the things that keeps the home planet habitable.

Q: What is the difference between steam and smoke?
A: Steam is water in the act of becoming invisible and smoke is a tiny crowd of solids unwilling to become anything at all. Steam is made when water boils; the white puff above a kettle is actually steam that has cooled back into mist, since true steam itself is clear. Smoke is made of particles of half-burned fuel riding up on the hot gases of a fire. You can wave steam from your face and it vanishes within inches; smoke follows you home and gets into your clothes to remind you of the bonfire at midnight.

Q: How would you explain gravity to a five-year-old?
A: I would say: the Earth is like a very patient mother who never lets go. Everything on Earth belongs to her, so she keeps it near — your feet, your cat, your cereal. When you drop a ball, the ball does not choose the floor; the floor is just the nearest part of a mother reaching out for everything at once. And I would add, because five-year-olds inspect the edges of all explanations: she reaches even as far as the moon, which is why the moon circles instead of wandering off.

Q: What is a solstice?
A: A solstice is one of the two days in the year when the tilt of the Earth leans most toward or away from the sun. On the summer solstice, the leaning-toward hemisphere gets its longest day and the sun climbs highest; on the winter solstice, the leaning-away hemisphere endures its shortest day and miserably low sun. Ancient people watched those turning points carefully, because they told you when to plant, when to store, and when to celebrate surviving another winter.

Q: Why does the moon have no weather?
A: The moon has no weather because it has almost no air. Weather is the stirring of an atmosphere by the sun, and the moon's gravity is too gentle to hold a real blanket of gas against the vacuum of space. A handful of stray atoms hug its surface, but no winds blow, no clouds gather, and no rain has ever fallen on the gray plains. An astronaut's footprint may wait there unbothered for a million years, with no wind to erase it.

Q: What is coral?
A: Coral is a colony of thousands of tiny animals, each no bigger than a rice grain, living on top of the limy skeletons of their grandparents. The animals, called polyps, are cousins of jellyfish, although stay-at-homes by comparison, and over centuries their collective limestone masonry builds the reefs that shelter a quarter of the ocean's fish. Inside each polyp live even tinier algae, which power the partnership with sunlight, which is why reefs need clear, shallow, well-lit water.

Q: How does aspirin find the pain?
A: Aspirin does not, in fact, find anything at all; pain relief is energetic but not directional. The drug dissolves, enters the blood, and travels everywhere impartially, and wherever it goes it slows down the making of certain chemicals the injured tissue had been using as alarms. With fewer alarm chemicals being released, the brain receives a quieter report. The medicine did not visit the sore knee; it quieted the alarm in every room of the house, and the knee merely reported the silence.

Q: What is the difference between a clock and a calendar?
A: A clock chops time into tiny equal seconds so we can coordinate a day; a calendar divides time into the big lumpy seasons, months, and days so we can coordinate a life. They answer different hungers. The clock tells you when to meet the train; the calendar tells you when to plant tomatoes. Both had to be invented, and both exist because humans found they had more to do with each other tomorrow than they could find each other for just by looking up.

Q: What would happen if insects all grew to the size of dogs?
A: The would-be giants would suffocate, because insects breathe through little tubes in their sides and have no lungs to pump air. That system works at ant scale and fails at dog scale: the atmosphere simply cannot reach the middle of a large enough body fast enough. So the horror films are safe, and nature stays politely small where insects are concerned — a mercy to picnickers everywhere.

Q: Who was Cleopatra?
A: Cleopatra was the last active ruler of ancient Egypt, a queen of Macedonian Greek descent who governed a kingdom older than Rome. She spoke many languages, managed a nation of farmers and scribes with extraordinary skill, and spent the end of her life tangled in Roman power struggles in which Rome, with its larger fleet, ultimately triumphed. Later ages flattened her into a portrait of beauty; the coins she minted show a stronger, cleverer, more interesting face than the legend prefers.

Q: How does a seed know when to sprout?
A: A seed is not guessing; it is reading instruments. It tests the temperature of the soil — is winter truly gone? It tests the moisture — is there enough water to begin? Some seeds even test the light or the smoke in the air, sprouting only after a fire has cleared the land. Sprouting is the most consequential decision a plant will ever make, and evolution has made the decision committees inside seeds very fussy. The sleeping walnut in your bowl is doing a great deal of listening at the table.
