There is a temptation, in any age that manufactures its tools faster than its wisdom, to dismiss cultivation of the mind as the ornament of the idle. The counter-argument is older than every library that survives: nothing else protects a people from being deceived. A nation that does not read widely may build roads — the Romans proved that — but it will believe anything that comes down the road with a confident voice.

This is not to say that reading makes a person good. It makes a person harder to make stupid against their will. The library shelf democratizes suspicion: a passage read in an afternoon can vaccinate against a decade of smooth deceit. And the library is the one institution in which the price of admission has always been curiosity, not money. One enters poor and leaves richer, which is a fair definition of wealth.

The case for libraries rests ultimately on the observation that human beings are learning animals first and economic animals second. When economics fails, we still read. When libraries fail, we forget what we were for.

Of the many habits considered virtuous in school, orderly behavior is the one most easily mistaken for education itself. A classroom can be silent and yet empty; a student can raise their hand at the correct height for fifteen years and be untouched by knowledge at the end of it. True instruction requires a milder, subtler disobedience: the willingness, at times, to read past the end of the chapter.

The purpose of a syllabus is to teach the syllabus; the purpose of education is the syllabus becomes unnecessary in the presence of a good question. Every student who has stayed up past lights-out with a contraband book understands this intuitively. A model pupil who obeys everything is career-ready; a slightly rebellious reader who obeys most things is future-ready. The gap between the two is the gap between preparation and curiosity, and curiosity, unlike preparation, is the quality that will still be needed in the room of all the jobs that do not yet exist.

Consider two proposals for improving a river town. One proposes to hold a festival in the town's honor every spring: a procession, a market, decorations on the town hall, with the budget presented by its supporting alderman as an investment in civic pride. The other proposes that the town clear the ditch along its flood road each spring, an equally annual expense, whose presentation by its supporting engineer as an investment in civic dryness attracts zero parade floats.

The first is more photographed; the second is more felt. A town that pays for ten festivals and one ditch-clearing a decade will remember fondly the last nine festivals, until the one flood that erases the word "fond." The logic of all public expenditure tends toward the festive and against the ditches, and yet every disaster report ends by quoting exactly that ditch of a year ago with ominous emphasis — as if its water had been waiting to be asked, which in a manner of speaking it had.

Infrastructure is how a community decides what it owes itself. It is the least romantic form of foresight, and the most needed.

The common defense of competition is that it makes us better; the common complaint is that it makes us worse. Both contain a portion of the truth. Competition improves performance, in the way that fire improves iron; but iron is not the only metal in the workshop, and there is such a metal as copper, which the same fire melts.

A gentler picture suggests that what competition provides is direction, while what practice provides is form. Together they produce accomplishment, separately they produce anxiety and stagnation respectively. The athlete training alone without competition becomes predictable; the athlete who competes daily without practice becomes exhausted and predictable. The ordinary worker may not be an Olympic sprinter, but the same arithmetic applies to the Saturday footballer, the choir member, the pastry apprentice — most lives require a small, bearable number of arenas. Enough to aim at; few enough to rest.

There exists in each of us a capacity to delay gratification which, when not exercised, grows smaller like an unused muscle, and, when exercised, grows larger by the same law. The evidence is not grand: the student who avoids the window while reading the final chapter; the parent who sets the alarm for half an hour before the household; the writer who turns off the evening's entertainment because the morning's paragraph must be paid first. None of these acts is heroic; their heroism is precisely that nobody sees them and nobody must.

The modern economy is built in part upon the manufacture of necessary distraction, and the result is that self-denial has become economically inconvenient. It is easier to rest the eyes on a device than on a paragraph. Yet the country — any country — still depends upon the habits that the eye reserves for the paragraph. Every accountant's precision, every surgeon's steadiness, every parent's infinite patience begins as a private refusal to check the news during the work at hand; and the work at hand is, in the end, the only work there is.

It is probably necessary to say, at the outset of a discussion of the printed book, that the printed book is a machine for the preservation of knowledge, and that every machine can be improved or replaced by a better machine; and that, if the better machine comes, the printed book will have to make way for it, not as a sentiment but as a practicality.

But let us also say that the book does something that no glowing screen has yet done better: it is finished. A printed book argues with the reader at the time of reading, from one position, with covers closed against the possibility of edit, revision, advertisement, or comment by the next reader. The book in the hand is the completed thought of a person at a desk. That completeness is not sentimentality — it is the reason every law firm in the world still uses paper for documents of consequence, and why every government prints its statutes even when it publishes them digitally.

Technology improves our instruments every decade. It improves our attention almost never. Until evidence proves otherwise, the deep book remains what it has been for five centuries: the safest known container for the slow passage of difficult ideas into ordinary minds.

A society can be measured by what its institutions expect of its citizens in the matter of keeping records. When a hospital keeps a ledger, it expects a reader later; when a court writes down what the witness said, it expects a future appeal; when a parent enters a child's height on a door jamb, they expect the child to grow, and want to see the change. Every record is a loan of information from the present to some future self.

The loan is repaid with interest when the future self does not remember the facts. Historians understand this; that is why they value the strange little lists (tax receipts, grain accounts, ships' manifests) more than the grand speeches of their eras: the lists are what actually happened, and the speeches are what people said happened. A family that keeps its photographs labeled and dated is building the same honest archive for its own descendants. No one thanks you for it until ninety years later, when they cannot stop thanking you in their silence.
